ABSTRACT

Trillions of cigarettes are smoked annually making cigarette butts one of the most common types of litter in the world. Due to the materials and toxic substances that they contain, this waste carries a very harmful risk for the environment and for living organisms (including humans). Only a few - barely sustainable - solutions have tried to tackle this waste and alternative solutions to landfilling and incineration are needed. Identifying the best methodological solutions and technologies for recycling this kind of waste in terms of results and applicability to real contexts would reduce the presence of dangerous materials in the environment and ecosystems and would promote the recovery of materials in line with the circular economy and sustainable development.

The objective of this review was to collect and analyze the alternative solutions available in the literature for the recovery and recycling of the materials in cigarette butts, considering them as possible sources of secondary raw materials applicable to contexts of common interest. Several papers were selected and the results obtained by the authors are presented in terms of type of treatment process (physical, chemical or both), product derived (in solid, liquid or gaseous form) and its possible use in different sectors (e.g. construction, electronics, energy, chemistry and environmental protection). The main results, together with the advantages and disadvantages are highlighted and proposals for further research are outlined.

Abbreviations: CF, cigarette filter “component of a cigarette specifically designed to absorb vapors and to accumulate particulate smoke components”. In this paper, the authors use CF as a clean component before its use during cigarette smoking; CB, cigarette butt “part of a cigarette that is left after it has been smoked”; CA, cellulose acetate “acetate ester of cellulose, also used in the manufacture of cigarette filters”

# 1. Introduction

The world faces numerous environmental challenges on a daily basis. Efforts to address them are aimed at facilitating sustainable and responsible growth.

As described by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2017), “Tobacco threatens many of the Earth’s resources”. The WHO study analyses the life cycle impacts of tobacco and shows how this product (and its industry) creates waste and inflicts damage on the environment throughout its life cycle. It also examines how this requires all those involved with tobacco control to think about how these consequences are linked to sustainable development goals.

This is a global challenge because, although the cultivation, harvesting and processing of tobacco are limited to specific areas of the world, the consumption and post-consumer waste are a problem that affects the entire planet (inhabited and uninhabited places). This is waste that can be found everywhere, which is often difficult to collect and even more complex to treat properly. Furthermore, the corresponding costs are not necessarily borne by the manufacturers, distributors, or users of tobacco products, but generally are the responsibility of all citizens.

Worldwide, an estimated 967 million daily smokers consumed approximately 6.25 trillion cigarettes over the course of 2012 (Ng et al., 2014). According to the Tobacco Atlas (https://tobaccoatlas.org), a project of the American Cancer Society and the Vital Strategies, in 2016 the number of cigarettes smoked decreased to 5.7 trillion. Despite this, the number is expected to reach nine trillion by 2025 due to further growth in the market (Mackay et al., 2006). This is mainly due to population growth (Srbinoska et al., 2018). The number of cigarettes per smoker per day also varies widely across countries (Ng et al., 2014).

Fig. 1 shows the global cigarette consumption per region between 1999 and 2016. The trend highlights the regions affected by higher growth (China and South-East Asia Region - SEARO) and those characterized by a decline (European Region - EURO, Americas - AMRO, Western Pacific Region - WPRO - excluding China). This trend is reflected in the per capita consumption and on the waste associated with cigarette consumption, as shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

CBs are the main type of litter: they are one of the most frequently collected waste items during clean-ups, in particular in beach and coastal environments (Araújoa and Costa, 2019; Ocean Conservancy, 2018). The estimated annually discarded waste from global cigarette consumption could be anywhere between 340–680 million kg, besides almost two million tonnes of paper, ink, cellophane, foil and glue that are used in tobacco product packaging. More than 40% of all items

collected during urban clean-ups and removed from public areas such as beaches and parks are CBs and related products (Bonanomi et al., 2015; Ariza et al., 2008). According to WHO (2017), up to two-thirds of every smoked cigarette is discarded on the ground, and about 76% according to Patel et al. (2013). This situation is explained by Patel et al. (2013) and Novotny and Zhao (1999): through interviews, they found that 73.5% of smokers do not use correct practices for the disposal of CBs, which are often therefore released into the environment.

According to Roder Green et al. (2014), CBs are not uniformly distributed in an urban area, but are strongly connected to the location of sales and consumption. Their research (concentrated in the US) showed evidence of a higher concentration in proximity to bars, convenience stores, liquor stores, cafes, gas stations, grocery stores, restaurants, and traffic signals, as also reported by Marah and Novotny (2011) and Van Gucht et al. (2010). Furthermore, the authors report how CBs are concentrated at places of transition from an outdoor to an indoor environment (entrances to buildings, vehicles or public transportation stations).

The big problem associated with this waste, in addition to its lack of control as it is freely dispersed in the environment, is that it is not biodegradable and that it contains and releases over 7000 toxic chemicals in the environment.

Its toxicity and non-biodegradability could justify environmental regulation and policies to raise the price of tobacco and further discourage its use (Smith and McDaniel, 2010).

However, practical operational aspects are lacking, in particular at the regulatory level. For example, the European List of Waste (EC, 2000; EU, 2014) does not propose a specific entry for CBs, which is therefore considered as “Municipal waste including separately collected fraction/Separately collected fraction/Other fractions not otherwise specified” (Rebischung et al., 2018).

Therefore, using a "precautionary principle" and appropriate awareness raising and accountability practices among consumers and producers would substantially contribute to reducing the production of waste and its dispersion into the environment.

Currently, given the poor results, finding effective management solutions for this waste is essential as it has been widely demonstrated how conventional disposal methods mainly represented by landfilling or incineration are neither universally sustainable nor economically feasible for this purpose (Bandi et al., 2018; Mohajerani et al., 2016). Identifying a recycling methodology for this kind of waste would reduce the release of dangerous materials into the environment and to the ecosystems, and would promote the recovery of materials in line with the circular economy and sustainable development.

## 2. Characteristics of cigarette filters and CBs

A cigarette is a small cylinder of finely cut tobacco leaves rolled in thin paper.

Modern commercially manufactured cigarettes are composed of the following main elements (Fig. 4):

* Tobacco blend dried and cut into thin one millimeter thick strips. The characteristics of tobacco change from manufacturer to manufacturer. Dangerous chemicals can form in and be deposited on tobacco during the processing of the tobacco leaves. Other dangerous chemicals are created when the tobacco filler is burned. Often there

are also various additives that manufacturers can add to make smoking more appealing and to mask the harshness of the smoke. Humectants such as propylene glycol or glycerol, as well as flavouring products and enhancers such as cocoa solids, liquorice, tobacco extracts, and various sugars are some of the additives in cigarettes. There are hundreds of possible additives, however these are not declared on the cigarette packages on the market.

* Rolling paper to cover the tobacco and adhesive. Composed of cellulose (with possible additives), this paper favours the appearance of the ash and guarantees a uniform combustion. It is closed by an adhesive substance.
* A filter made up of cellulose acetate, described in more detail below.
* Tipping paper to cover the filter. This is a polyvinyl acetate emulsion which is used to glue the cellulose acetate plug to the inner wrapping paper.

According to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (USDHHS), smokers are exposed to a toxic mix of over 7000 chemicals when they inhale cigarette smoke (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (USDHHS, 2010). Hundreds of these substances are toxic, about 70 are carcinogenic.

In 2012 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) published a preliminary list of 93 Harmful and Potentially Harmful Constituents (HPHCs) that are linked to the five most serious health effects of tobacco use (cancer, cardiovascular disease, respiratory effects, reproductive problems, and addiction). An important element that makes up the cigarette is the filter (CF). CFs were introduced in the middle of the 20th century, based on a patent by Boris Aivaz recorded in 1925.

A filter is designed to reduce the amount of smoke, tar and fine particles inhaled during the combustion and to reduce the harshness of the smoke and keep tobacco flakes out of the smoker's mouth. The filter captures and retains toxic substances, but when littered, such chemicals can leach out of the cigarette filter.

According to Mohajerani et al. (2016), almost 90% of all cigarettes are manufactured with cellulose acetate filter tips except in Japan, Venezuela, South Korea and Hungary.

CFs are made of about 95% poorly degradable microscopic-sized white fibres massed together (about 12,000 per filter with about 30,000 dernier), characterized by a Y-shaped cross-section that is not perpendicular to the flow (the angle ranges from 18° to 49°) (Du et al., 2015) and it is made of CA. These fibres are synthetic plastic which looks like cotton. In the CFs, the CA fibres are linked to each other through the glycerol triacetate, which is a plasticizer (Hamzah and Umar, 2017). CA is one of the most important esters of cellulose. It is produced through a two-step process, involving the esterification of acetic acid with cellulose and the partial hydrolysis of the resulting ester groups. CA can be produced with varying degrees of substitution. The most common form of cellulose acetate fibre has an acetate group on approximately 2–2.5 of every three hydroxyls (Fischer et al., 2008), due to good solubility in common solvents, molecular weights, and melt properties (Puls et al., 2011). CA has various applications which depend on its processing and its physico-chemical characteristics. In particular, it is used for the production of textiles, plastics, films, and CFs.

The common name for the remains of a cigarette after smoking is a CB. According to Poppendieck et al. (2016), a CB can be defined as “the cigarette remaining at the conclusion of the smouldering phase following smoking. The conclusion of the smouldering phase is defined as when the entire cigarette butt reaches the ambient temperature of the environment in which it is located. The CB includes any remaining paper (wrapping paper, tipping paper, and plug wrap paper)”. It is typically about 30% of the cigarette's original length.

The three major CB components that Poppendieck et al. (2016) listed are: 1) ash, 2) unburned tobacco and 3) filtration material (Fig. 6).

A filter, after its use, has absorbed smoke, trap tar, toxic chemicals and particle smoke. Its chemical composition depends on the type of tobacco used, the cigarette paper, category and effectiveness of the filter as well as the degree of tip ventilation. It also depends on the filter design. Strain et al. (2015) analyzed the smoke filtering properties of different fibres using a cigarette as the smoke source. The tests showed the presence of many carcinogenic hydrocarbons and alcohols that exist in tobacco smoke. Experiments have also shown that the original CA cigarette filter tip absorbed only 2.7 times its own weight during the same treatment.

The main problem associated with CBs is their slow degradation rate and the high concentration of toxic content.

Typically, cellulose acetate possesses poor photo- and bio-degradable properties, making CBs persistent in the environment. The methods and times required for this process depend on the degradation mechanism, of which there are different types, e.g. photodegradation, biodegradation, and mechanical processes or a combination of these as typically occurs in outdoor environments due to different factors. Some researchers have demonstrated how the speed of degradation processes are inversely proportional to time. In particular, Puls et al. (2011) carried out a review of degradation mechanisms and the potential degradation rates of CA. According to Puls et al. (2011), cellulose is degradable thanks to organisms that utilize cellulose enzymes. CA, due to the additional acetyl groups, requires the presence of esterases for the first step in degradation. Also, the high molecular weight, crystallinity and the physical form influence the microbial degradation efficacy (Haske-Cornelius et al., 2017; Ogundare et al., 2017). Furthermore, CA is photo-chemically degraded by UV, but its property is limited to photo degradability in sunlight due to the lack of chromophores for absorbing ultraviolet light (Robertson et al., 2012). Thus cellulose acetate-based cigarette filters do not biodegrade under most circumstances because of their compressed make up and the presence of acetyl molecules (Haske-Cornelius et al., 2017).

CBs require several years to degrade completely (Bonanomi et al., 2015; Puls et al., 2011). According to Kadir et al. (2009), the degrading rates vary from within 1 to 2 months under anaerobic conditions, 6 to 9 months in soil, 12 months in fresh water and 36 months or more under poor conditions, for example, sea water, desert and underground stations. Bonanomi et al. (2015) has demonstrated this slow process of degradation through a 2-year experiment in a standard decomposing organic substrate. The study shows how CBs lost only 37.8% of their initial mass after two years of decomposition. Comparing cellulose and plastic cigarette filter decomposition, Joly and Coulis (2018) estimated that conventional plastic filters take 7.5–14 years to disappear in compost and on the soil surface, respectively. In contrast, they estimated that cellulose filters take 2.3–13 years to disappear, in compost and on the soil surface, respectively.

The other significant problem is the over 7000 toxic chemicals (including up to 50 human carcinogens according to Hoffmann and Hoffmann (1997), toxic heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, pesticides, N-nitrosamines, aromatic amines, formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, benzene and nicotine) that this waste releases into the water, the soil, and the air, and which then comes into direct contact with humans, animals and vegetation.

Poppendieck et al. (2016) provided a very in-depth review of the chemicals in cigarette butt emission and extraction studies, divided by media (air, water, extract solution, cigarette filter, cigarette butt), which summarized the chemicals found in the environment are a potential threat to human health and the environment, especially marine ecosystems. These problems, in particular associated with harmful chemicals, have been analysed by several other authors.

Roder Green et al. (2014) studied the effects of the nicotine contained in CBs on urban water resources through batch and rainfall experiments. Slaughter et al. (2011) analysed the effects on fish exposed to CBs soaked in either fresh or salt water. Singh and Kathiresan (2015) analysed the effects on fish and microbial communities. Oropesa et al. (2017) studied the effects on aquatic organisms from different trophic levels. This process revealed that nicotine poses an important risk to aquatic organisms. Booth et al. (2015) tested how common intertidal molluscs were affected by butt leachate.

Slaughter et al. (2011) showed that percolates from CBs are toxic to a range of marine organisms. The LC50 (lethal concentration capable of killing 50% of the animals used in the test) is 1 CB/l for both marine and freshwater species.

## 3. Methodology

This paper reviews the recent studies on CB recycling solutions. The scientific literature contains many interesting examples; however, the present review focuses only on specific experiences of CBs, which used this waste as input for physical and/or chemical treatment in order to obtain second raw materials, semi-finished products and finished goods applicable in industrial, agricultural, civil engineering, environmental or chemical contexts.

The research was conducted on papers published prior to 1 January 2019 using ScienceDirect and Scopus databases. The keywords used were: “Cigarette Butt”, “Cigarette Filter” and “Cigarette Tip”. These keywords were searched for individually or coupled with the term "recycling" using the "*" wildcard character used for multi-word searches. The results of the search, expressed as the number of papers identified, are summarized in Table 1.

Despite the numerous papers available, most were excluded from this work as they were not related to the topic discussed in this document. In fact, CBs are the subject of numerous related studies, for example, assessing environmental impacts and ecotoxicity, pollutant emission rates, physical dispersion, health effects, etc.

The papers identified were further filtered by selecting only those conforming to the focus of the present study: articles that directly studied possible ways for the recovery and recycling of CBs.

A total of 37 papers were thus selected which consider the definition of CBs, their chemical composition, techniques to treat and purify the smoked CBs, the products and materials that can be obtained and the performances of the products obtained.

The papers selected were classified using criteria that describe the process input, sample treatment system, output and possible use. The geographical distribution of the studies was also considered. The five criteria used were (Fig. 7):

1. Input for recycling: identifies the types of samples used as input to the treatment process. CBs are differentiated according to the filter production and characteristics of the smoked tobacco. Typically, this type of study compares CBs smoked with new and unused CFs (Table 2). Few authors used filters smoked in the laboratory to mimic the smoking process.
2. Methodology/Treatment system: identifies the type of approach adopted for the treatment of samples, identifying methods of chemical treatment, physical treatment and physical-chemical treatment. A brief description is also provided aimed at highlighting the methodological steps used by the authors (Table 3).
3. Type of product obtained from the process: characterizes the type of

product obtained from the treatment process, identifying its physical state be it a solid, liquid or gaseous product. This criterion characterizes the output of the treatment process (Table 4).

4. Possible uses: describes the results of the process and describes its possible use. This criterion characterizes the possible use of the output obtained (Table 5).
5. Geographical distribution: identifies the country where the study was conducted (Table 6).

The results obtained from the evaluation of each criterion are described in the Section 4.

## 4. Results

Considering the final objective of the present study and assessing the results of recent studies on CB recycling solutions, the outcomes of the review are presented by individually analysing the criteria identified and described in Section 4.

### 4.1. Sample input

The reviewed papers are classified in Table 2 according to the type of CBs used as input to the treatment and recycling process.

The analysed papers apply approaches and techniques using smoked or unsmoked filters. The use of one or the other, or a comparison of the performance of the type of input used, depends on the authors' methodological approach.

Of the 37 selected contributions, most of the authors (about 97%) used smoked CBs to develop and test recycling solutions. Only two cases (about 3%) used mimicked smoked CBs (one author) or supplementary materials (one author) as a precursor to the treatment and recovery process. Only five papers (about 14%) proposed a comparison using unused CFs. This is important because it checks whether and how the recycling process could influence the results, comparing the use of filters to "pure" material (cellulose acetate).

This comparison was conducted by Blankenship and Mokaya (2017), who used cigarette filters / butts to form the precursor for porous carbons in order to evaluate their hydrogen storage capacity. The results showed that a sequential benign hydrothermal carbonisation and activation treatment obtained a super-porous and ultra-high surface area (4300 m² g⁻¹) and pore volume (2.09 cm³ g⁻¹) arising almost entirely (> 90%) from micropores. These characteristics, combined with the oxygen-rich nature of the material, demonstrated the highest hydrogen storage capacity reported in all the papers analysed. The comparison between smoked and unused filters highlighted various anomalies of the smoked samples mainly due to the presence of heavy metals.

Polarz et al. (2002) studied the transformation of cellulose acetate filters into micro-meter or nano-meter porous carbon structures. Applying an easy direct process, filters from smoked or unsmoked cigarettes were transformed into non-graphitic carbon (defined by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry as “all varieties of solids consisting mainly of the element carbon with two-dimensional long-range order of the carbon atoms in planar hexagonal networks, but without any measurable crystallographic order in the third direction (c-direction) apart from more or less parallel stacking” and with many concrete applications such as heat shields, Li-ion batteries, activated carbons). The resulting material exhibited a well-defined mesoporosity, with different characteristics from the cellulose acetate fibres used as a precursor and with a good potential application in the field of gas purification or more generally as filters.

Gómez Escobar and Maderuelo Sanz (2017) analyzed the performance of smoked and unused CBs as absorbing materials. Each sample was obtained by collecting 10 butts inside an impedance tube. The samples differed in the type of butt used and their physical characteristics (length and diameter). All showed a high sound absorption---

The solution of using by-products resulting from the treatment processes of CBs in the construction industry and infrastructure has the greatest support among the authors analysed, with 31% of studies describing this possible use.

This research area has been extensively studied by Mohajerani et al. (2016); Kadir and Mohajerani (2015); Sarani and Kadir (2013); Kadir and Mohajerani (2011); Kadir et al. (2009) and Kadir and Mohajerani (2008) and reviewed by Bories et al. (2014).

They evaluated the use of CBs as a mixed component in the preparation of fired bricks, which is considered as common solution to pollution and is already applied with other waste (e.g. rubber, limestone dust and wood sawdust, processed waste tea, fly ash, polystyrene, and sludge).

These authors demonstrated that adding different concentrations of CBs to the compound (from 0% to 10% by weight and corresponding to about 0–30% by volume) for the production of fired bricks, leads to a reduction in the density of the fired bricks, an increase in porosity, and a decrease in thermal conductivity. Low-density or light-weight bricks have several advantages over standard bricks (lower structural dead load, easier handling, lower transport costs, different thermal conductivity), except when stronger bricks are needed. However, this method also leads to a reduction in compressive strength (although in the case studies, the results were lower than the acceptable regulatory Australian limits) which is also important when considering their use. With controlled temperatures, it is also possible to reduce the energy usage during firing and gas emissions into the atmosphere. The related atmospheric emissions have been studied in detail through experimental tests that compared the Estimated Total Emission (ETE) of different types of bricks produced at different heating rates during firing (Mohajerani et al., 2016 and Kadir and Mohajerani, 2015).

These results demonstrate how CBs can be considered as a potential addition to raw materials used in the manufacturing of light fired bricks.

Another use analyzed by the authors is related to the production of asphalt for streets and as prefabricated concrete paving. Mohajerani et al. (2017) studied the possibility of incorporating CBs into asphalt concrete for pavement construction, encapsulating CBs with different classes of bitumen and paraffin wax. Encapsulation prevents chemical translocation from CBs to the environment. Comparing the results obtained in the two cases with the control asphalt concrete sample (no CBs), using mechanical and volumetric properties, including stability, flow, resilient modulus, bulk density, maximum density, air voids, and voids in mineral aggregates as variables, the proposed solutions were found to satisfy the requirements for light, medium and heavy traffic conditions for bitumen samplers, while paraffin wax only satisfied the light traffic conditions for road pavements.

The authors identified this solution as a useful contribution to the decrease in urban heat effect in urban environments due to the reduction in bulk density, the increase in porosity and the reduction of thermal conductivity

A similar study was also carried out by Cui et al. (2018) and Wadalkar et al. (2018). They analyzed the possible benefits of incorporating CBs into precast concrete blocks for paving, assessing their performance as the percentage of added CB increases. They observed that the compressive strength decreased with an increase in the proportion of CBs, while flexural strength and water absorption increased.

To exploit the sound insulation capabilities of CBs, Maderuelo-Sanz et al. (2018) and Gómez Escobar and Maderuelo Sanz (2017) demonstrated the high sound absorption performance of this material and the potential use of CBs as a component for insulating solutions, which were found to be highly competitive even with commercial solutions.

In the field of environmental engineering, the products deriving from the recycling of CBs are above all applied in the treatment of water for oil / water separation, exploiting the absorbing power of CA obtained from CBs. Similar applications have been described by several authors (14% of the total analyzed). Often, the proposed solutions are applicable to accidental conditions, with the release of oil, methylene blue (MB) or liquid petroleum derivatives. Among the various solutions (natural, chemical, and mechanical remediation), the use of oil-absorbent materials is one of the most economical and effective approaches, due to the low operational costs, easy operation and high efficiency. The tests performed have shown very promising results:

* 25.6, 15.8, 17.2, and 21.5 g/g for vegetable oil, diesel oil, motor oil, and vacuum pump (VP) oil, respectively and the sorbent oil sorption capacity did not significantly deteriorate after 20 cycles of reuse (Ifelebuegu et al., 2018).
* maximum monolayer adsorption capacities of 0.3, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.5 g/g (Lima et al., 2018).
* adsorption capacities between 30.6 g/g and 38.3 g/g and high adsorption capacity after 10 cycles (Xiong et al., 2018a).
* adsorption capacities for (MB) of 88.76 m²/g (Hamzah and Umar, 2017).

The use of CBs for energy purposes has also proved to be of interest in the literature. A total of 19% of the authors analyzed reported research on energy, particularly in the field of new, clean, and high-efficiency energy storage devices.

Xiong et al. (2018b) studied the use of CBs as a precursor for the development of supercapacitors, which are one of the best potential energy storage systems thanks to higher energy and power densities than conventional capacitors. They developed a new application of CBs for energy storage, producing carbon with a large specific capacitance and good capacitance retention through the combination of CBs and polypyrrole (PPy). PPy is a conducting polymer used as an electrode material for supercapacitors. The excellent storage performance obtained by the authors was due to the synergistic effects of the porous backbone structure derived from CBs and the pseudocapacitance behavior of PPy-derived nitrogen functionality.

Lee et al. (2014) also developed a supercapacitor based on CBs as the active material, producing a nitrogen doped (N-doped) meso-/microporous hybrid carbon material (NCF), which is a porous carbon material that spontaneously contains both mesopores and micropores. The results highlighted a good capability and higher specific capacitance during 6000 charge and discharge cycles.

In relation to hydrogen, as an attractive energy source especially for motor vehicles, Blankenship and Mokaya (2017) demonstrated that porous carbons derived from CBs with a super porous, ultra-high surface area and pore volume can reach for storing hydrogen. The combination of high surface area, high microporosity and the oxygen-rich nature of the carbons material, ensures a storage capacity of 8.1 wt% excess uptake, and 9.4 wt% total uptake at -196 °C and 20 bar, rising to a total uptake of 10.4 wt% and 11.2 wt% at 30 and 40 bar, respectively.

Kim et al. (2016) developed highly efficient catalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction based on CBs. This is a bimodal porous nitrogen (N) doped carbon supported Pt composite for the industrial development of fuel cells.

Ghosh et al. (2017) showed how pyrolysis treatment produces a material with good conductive properties which can be further used in various electronic fields.

Wang et al. (2016) used waste metal ions and CBs at the same time to develop a hybrid electrode material of carbon fiber and vanadium nitride (CF@VN) with a hierarchical porous structure. The geographical distribution of the studies (Fig. 9) shows a strong concentration in Asia, Central and South America and in Australia. Europe is represented by Spain and the UK. Few authors are from North America or Africa.

## 5. Conclusions and prospects for development

Despite cigarette butts (CBs) being one of the most common types of litter worldwide, with a production of one million tonnes each year, the solution to the problem still appears very challenging and distant.

In fact, this involves at least two main aspects:

1. Consumers: increasing awareness of the environmental impact of CBs and changing their habits in dealing with this waste (today in most cases still discarded on the ground). Policies and awareness campaigns are used to achieve this.
2. Management methodologies, identifying one (or more) economical and effective technological solutions for the prevention or treatment of this waste and also identifying the best approaches for the collection and transport of CBs.

This paper has analysed the second aspect through a general review of 37 selected papers that provide different types of solutions for the treatment and recycling of CBs.

The following conclusions can be drawn from the results presented in this paper:

* CBs constitute widespread waste globally and are often more concentrated in areas with the highest number of consumers. China is an example of this situation: people smoke over 40% of all cigarettes globally (2043 cigarettes per person per year) and has the largest number of CBs and packaging waste in the world.
* This aspect also influences the origin of the studies analysed, which are often concentrated in areas where the problem appears to be very significant or, as in the case of India and Mexico, where local conditions require dedicated solutions (e.g. integrated vector management strategies).
* Comparing the authors' approaches to the treatment of CBs, in most tests only smoked CBs were used, often without providing comparative analyses of the same non-smoked material (CFs cleaned).
* The preparation and treatment methods of the samples are mainly of a physical nature, through which the CBs are separated from the remaining non-smoked tobacco, and from the outer paper and crushed into small fragments.
* The results of the treatment processes constitute in particular solid materials used in numerous economic and productive sectors, especially in the field of building materials, energy and the environment and chemistry.
* Few authors describe the methods of collecting the CB samples. In fact, laboratory applications do not require large quantities of samples. However, the logistical aspect associated with the collection and handling of CBs is an extremely critical element of the process, which risks making the product developed ineffective and the economic system not economically viable.
* The treatment processes described all take place on a laboratory pilot scale. However, a large-scale analysis or experimental application is lacking, which would simulate the operational process with greater quantities and with the critical, operational, economic and legislative barriers that characterize a pilot case.
* With the exception of very few studies, most are very recent scientific contributions. This shows how the correct management of CBs represents a current priority of global interest.
* All the studies analysed describe the methodological and operative treatments of CBs and analyse the results and the products obtained in detail. This provides very detailed information on the characteristics of the resulting materials and performance under operating conditions. However, no author provides detailed information regarding the economic sustainability of the process or how to make these products suitable for the market.
* Finally, the review highlights that social aspects are still poorly investigated and, in most cases, neglected. Including social factors---

Therefore, the main challenge of preventing the production and spread of this waste remains.

Avoiding the production of waste according to the "precautionary principle" remains the optimal solution that must be increasingly applied and disseminated (WHO, 2017). The application of this principle is not simple, therefore solutions are also needed to avoid dispersion, that would facilitate the correct collection and management of CBs.

Below are some examples of possible measures to reduce the production and dispersion of CBs in the environment:

* Labeling the danger. Just as health risks are reported on packets, this information could also state that CBs and packaging products are a non-biodegradable hazardous waste. A description of the main effects on the environment could be included together with some recommendations on the correct disposal methods to reduce their dispersion.
* Using biodegradable materials and banning disposable filters. The use of biodegradable materials for the production of CFs and packaging products could be encouraged. There are already various international initiatives that reduce the impact and increase the sustainability of the box, inner liner, ink, filter, tobacco and cigarette paper.
* A "Deposit / Return" approach. This approach has already been successfully applied to other materials, for example glass bottles. The same approach, which includes a deposit for the purchase, could also be applied to the sale of cigarette packets. The "butt deposit" could be refunded when the packet is returned to the vender with the butts.
* A waste tax. A specific tax could be introduced that is added to the cost of cigarettes and used to finance specific activities, such as cleaning up local areas, financing research projects, and so on.
* Anti-smoking policies and waste fines. This solution requires political intervention by local administrations that could ban smoking in certain critical areas. The penalty for offenders would be a fine. This solution has already been tested: for example in 2019 several Italian beaches have been smoke-free. This prevents the presence and accumulation of CBs in critical areas, such as coastal areas.
* Litigation against the tobacco industry. This solution requires specific even economic interventions of the tobacco industries to promote solutions to the production and dispersion of CBs in the environment. Often, expensive legal battles are needed to obtain the support of the industry.
