ABSTRACT

Cigarette butts, which are usually thrown on the ground or into ordinary bins, have been recognized as toxic residues since may contain cigarette contaminants and chemicals produced during combustion. Therefore, contaminants in cigarette butts can be leached by rain into surface water and thereby contaminate the environment. In Brazil, according to the National Policy on Solid Waste, all residues must be disposed of in an environmentally friendly manner. Although cigarette butts are not mentioned in the law, due to their characteristics, they may be classified as hazardous waste. At the University of Brasilia, a cellulose pulp production process from cigarette butts has been developed employing alkaline pulping. This process is presented as an alternative to environmentally friendly final disposal of this residue. During the process, a dark liquor is generated, which was found to contain lignin, carbonyls, metals, nicotine and specific tobacco nitrosamines. The dark liquor was treated by acidification to promote lignin precipitation, coagulation with chitosan and $Al_2(SO_4)_3$ to remove metals and organic compounds and ozonated to oxidize resistant chemicals. The dark liquor presented a high chemical oxygen demand (COD; 29,986 mg/L), which was partially removed by precipitation (20%), chitosan coagulation (66%) and ozonation (45.8%). As the remaining COD was still high, we proposed reusing the clarified effluent in alkaline pulping, which seemed to be the easiest and most efficient procedure with the lowest cost.

1. Introduction

It is known that there are about 5000 constituents in cigarette smoke, which are distilled from tobacco or generated during burning. Among these chemicals, at least 150 of which 44 are present in large quantities in smoke, are recognized as being highly toxic, mainly because of their carcinogenic and mutagenic potential (Hoffmann et al., 1990). To diminish the absorption of these chemicals in the body, as well as their emission into the air, modern cigarettes have cellulose acetate filters, which allow for safer use by reducing or even eliminating those compounds from the smoke. Indeed, these chemicals are partially or fully retained by the cellulose acetate filter, allowing for somewhat safer cigarette consumption.

However, the consumption of cigarettes containing cellulose acetate filters generates tons of toxic cigarette butts annually, which are usually discarded on the streets of cities and are responsible for delivering toxic compounds into soils and limnic water. Indeed, the cellulose acetate filters of cigarette butts are saturated

with toxic compounds, unburned tobacco, ash and paper, and some researchers have highlighted them as environmental pollutants because of inadequate disposal, as butts are typically discarded into ordinary trash and on the ground (Barnes, 2011; Moerman and Potts, 2011; Novotny et al., 2009; Slaughter et al., 2011). Thus, these compounds can contaminate soil and be leached by rain water and brought to surface water and contaminate aquatic environments.

These wastes are small, but are generated in large quantities. For instance, according to the *Anuário Brasileiro do Tabaco*, the consumption of cigarettes in Brazil was about 89.1 billion in 2012 (Poll, 2013). Considering the cigarette butt average weight of 0.4 g, about 35,500 t of this residue is generated annually in Brazil. In the same year, worldwide consumption of cigarettes was about 5.8 trillion, of which about 2.6 trillion were consumed in China, representing about 2,320,000 t of cigarette butts discarded in the environment.

In Brazil, the National Policy on Solid Waste was instituted by Law 12305/2010 (Brasil, 2010), which regulates solid waste disposal and segregation according to the composition of the residue. According to Law 12305/2010, cigarette butts can be classified as municipal and hazardous waste and their segregation and disposal

In order to promote the environmentally friendly disposal of cigarette butts, our research group has developed a technology to recycle them for cellulose pulp production (PI 0305004-1 A) (Costa et al., 2005). The technology consists of separating all the cellulose content in the cigarette butt using four different processes: (i) dissolving the ash and all chemicals adsorbed in the filter; (ii) hydrolyzing cellulose acetate into cellulose; (iii) removing lignin from the remaining tobacco and liberating its cellulose; and (iv) disaggregation of the remaining paper. After filtering the resulting mixture, we obtained a cellulose pulp, which can be used in the paper industry, and a viscous dark liquor, which requires treatment. In the present work, we describe this method of cigarette butt processing, the characterization of the cellulose pulp, the analysis of the dark liquor and its possible treatments.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Cellulose pulp production

Cigarette butts were collected from cigarette butt collectors located in restaurants, bars and buildings from the Campus of the University of Brasilia and surroundings. The starting mixture contained cigarette butts from different blends and with different amounts of remaining tobacco and paper. Wastes from filter production in the cigarette industry were also used, which contained only cellulose acetate and paper. For the reaction, NaOH was used at a 1% concentration in a water solution to catalyze the reaction. The pulping or cooking process for each fiber (cigarette butts and cellulose acetate) occurred in a period of 3 h, at boiling temperature, in a pilot-scale stainless steel reactor with a 50 L capacity. The reactor was heated with a flame of liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) and the temperature was monitored by a digital thermocouple. A flowchart of the overall process is shown in Fig. 1.

Samples of pulps obtained from cigarette butts and cigarette filter wastes were sent to the Instituto de Pesquisas Tecnológicas (IPT) in São Paulo, Brazil, to evaluate the physical and optical parameters of the cellulose for commercial use as paper. The samples were refined in a Jokro mill for 25 min and the extent of refining was determined by the Schopper-Riegler degree (°SR). In addition, sheets from these samples were processed in TAPPI (Technical Association of Pulp and Paper Industry) forming equipment in order to evaluate the weight, thickness, brightness, tensile strength and elongation. In Table 1 are listed the standard methods used.

We also evaluated the possibility of mineralizing the organic matter by ozonation. Indeed, ozonation was carried out as a pre-oxidation treatment (directly in the dark liquor) and as an effluent post-oxidation process (in the precipitation and coagulation supernatants). The ozonation tests were performed using an installation adapted from Guimarães et al. (2010) and Houshyar et al. (2012), depicted in Fig. 2. An air compressor (Millipore, model WP6111560) fed a mini ozone generator (Di Acqua, model Di Acqua Flex$\text{O}_3/\text{ClO}_2$). The gas flow from the ozone generator was adjusted to 0.5 L/min and its ozone content (mass flow) was determined as 1 mg/min by titration, as described by standard method

SMWW 2350D (APHA, 1999). The ozone/air flow from the ozone generator was forced into a sequence of three traps, as described in Fig. 2. In the first trap, the ozone was bubbled through a bubble diffuser into the effluent (150 mL) under constant magnetic stirring. The exhaust from the first trap was directed into the second trap, which was empty, in order to collect any liquid or foam swept along by the current. Finally, the gas was bubbled into a 2% (v/v) KI solution (200 mL) in the third trap. After titration of this KI solution, according to standard method SMWW 2350D, it was possible to quantify the unreacted ozone, and, thus, by mass balance, approximately determine the amount of ozone that reacted with the effluent. The ozonation tests took place over a period of 4 h and samples were collected every hour to measure the pH and temperature and perform COD analysis. This procedure was carried out using the following effluents: (1) the dark liquor, (2) clarified effluent and (3) supernatant from the best coagulation assay. For each sample, tests were conducted at different starting pH levels: 9, 10, 11 and 12. For samples (2) and (3), it was necessary to add 0.5 mL of Aldrich Antifoam A5633 defoamer solution (1 mL of antifoam dissolved in 30 mL of propylene glycol).

## 3. Results and discussion

### 3.1. Cellulose pulp production

Cigarette butts contains four different components: (i) a filter made from cellulose acetate with a myriad of chemicals adsorbed

in it, (ii) unburned tobacco, (iii) ash and (iv) paper. In order to develop an environmentally friendly final disposal method for cigarette butts, a process was proposed to produce cellulose pulp, which can be used in the recycled paper market. Indeed, the main idea was to hydrolyze cellulose acetate into cellulose in the presence of strong alkali, depicted in Fig. 3. The use of a strong base may also remove lignin from unburned tobacco in the cigarette butt and thus liberate the cellulose from this residue, as well as dissolve the ash and disaggregate the remaining paper. Thus, at the end of the process, cellulose was obtained as a solid phase as well as a solution containing all the non-cellulose components of the cigarette butt.

Fig. 4 shows the FT-IR spectra obtained after cooking cigarette butts at 80 °C under different conditions: using distilled water for 6 h; NaOH solution (0.5%) for 10 min, 20 min and 150 min; and NaOH solution (1%) for 10 min. When cigarette butts were cooked using distilled water, no significant change in the FT-IR spectra was observed, even after 6 h (Fig. 4, curve 'a'). As shown in Fig. 4, it is possible to note a strong band at 1760 cm⁻¹ and a shoulder at 1730 cm⁻¹, corresponding to C=O stretching from the acetate group, strongly suggesting the preservation of cellulose acetate. However, when the cigarette butts were cooked in NaOH solution (0.5%), even for short times (Fig. 4, curve 'b'), it was possible to observe a decrease in the band at 1760 cm⁻¹, as well as in the shoulder at 1730 cm⁻¹; these features completely disappeared

## 3.2. Dark liquor characterization and treatment

It is worth mentioning that the collected cigarette butts were a very heterogeneous mixture. Indeed, it was possible to find cigarette butts from dozens of brands, each one with its specific original tobacco and blend of additives, and different amounts of remaining unburnt tobacco, as well as other possible contaminants, such as lipstick and saliva residue. Thus, it is reasonable to expect different chemical components in the dark liquor according to the starting mixture of cigarette butts. In order to study the dark liquor generated during the alkaline cooking of cigarette butts, 35 experiments were done with different starting materials; samples were taken after cooling and filtering the final mixture.

First, the main effluent parameters were determined and are presented in Table 5 as the average values obtained from the 35 dark liquor samples in comparison with the effluent release conditions stipulated by Brazilian law. As can be noted from the results presented in Table 5, the dark liquor had on average a very high organic content, even higher than those values usually observed in domestic sewage.

### 3.3. Cellulose pulp production from processed cigarette butts

Since we could not efficiently reduce the COD in cigarette butt cooking effluent, we aimed to develop an environmental friendly process to reuse the clarified effluent after acidifying the dark liquor to pH 3. Therefore, we propose that, after decantation of the precipitate, the clarified effluent should be collected and then, after adjusting its pH to 7, used in a new pulping process, as shown in Fig. 10.

We repeated the process seven times. Fig. 11 shows the dark liquor samples and the clarified effluent in each cycle. As can be seen in Fig. 11, the appearance of the samples remained almost constant after all cycles.

Table 11 presents the COD values for each cycle of effluent reuse. As shown in Table 11, no change in the pH of dark liquor was observed, since the clarified effluent had the same pH corrected by the additional water used for the pulping process. Regarding COD, an increase was observed for the initial reuses, but we noted that the COD value stabilized from the fifth reuse on.

The process developed here was licensed to a recycling company (Poiato Recicla, Sao Paulo, Brazil), which has started up a pilot plant in March, 2016. It is worth mentioning that they have operated the process several times, always reusing the clarified effluent, and have already processed some tons of cigarette butts. Indeed, they are obtaining a good quality cellulose pulp which is sold to a cardboard factory.

## 4. Conclusions

In summary, we have shown that is possible to produce cellulose pulp from cigarette butts. The produced cellulose pulp may

be absorbed by the recycling paper industry to be used pure or blended with pulps from various origins according to the quality requirements of the final paper. Thus, the process proposed here consists of an environmentally friendly solution for this solid residue which is generated in large quantities throughout the world. The dark liquor produced as the effluent in the pulping process contains a very high resistant organic content, including toxic nitrogenated compounds. Even after three treatment processes (precipitation by acidification, coagulation and ozonation), it was not possible to reduce the COD value of the effluent to an acceptable value. However, it was possible to reuse the effluent after one treatment step (precipitation) in the pulping process, thereby eliminating any liquid effluent from the overall process. Finally, it is worth mentioning that the process developed here was licensed to a recycling company, which has recently started up a pilot plant.
