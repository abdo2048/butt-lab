ABSTRACT
Trillions of cigarettes are smoked annually making cigarette butts one of the most common types of litter in the
world. Due to the materials and toxic substances that they contain, this waste carries a very harmful risk for the
environment and for living organisms (including humans). Only a few - barely sustainable - solutions have tried
to tackle this waste and alternative solutions to landfilling and incineration are needed. Identifying the best
methodologicalsolutions and technologiesforrecycling thiskind of wastein terms ofresults andapplicability to
real contexts would reduce the presence of dangerous materials in the environment and ecosystems and would
promote the recovery of materials in line with the circular economy and sustainable development.
The objective of this review was to collect and analyze the alternative solutions available in the literature for
the recovery and recycling of the materials in cigarette butts, considering them as possible sources of secondary
raw materials applicable to contexts of common interest. Several papers were selected and the results obtained
by the authors are presented in terms of type of treatment process (physical, chemical or both), product derived
(in solid, liquid or gaseous form) and its possible use in different sectors (e.g. construction, electronics, energy,
chemistry and environmental protection). The main results, together with the advantages and disadvantages are
highlighted and proposals for further research are outlined.

1. Introduction
The world faces numerous environmental challenges on a daily
basis. Efforts to address them are aimed at facilitating sustainable and
responsible growth.
As described by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2017),
“Tobacco threatens many of the Earth’s resources”. The WHO study
analyses the life cycle impacts of tobacco and shows how this product
(and its industry) createswaste andinflicts damage on the environment
throughout its life cycle. It also examines how this requires all those
involved with tobacco control to think about how these consequences
are linked to sustainable development goals.
This is a global challenge because, although the cultivation, har-
vesting and processing of tobacco are limited to specific areas of the
world, the consumption and post-consumer waste are a problem that
affects the entire planet (inhabited and uninhabited places). This is
waste that can be found everywhere, which is often difficult to collect
and even more complex to treat properly. Furthermore, the corre-
sponding costs are not necessarily borne by the manufacturers, dis-
tributors, or users of tobacco products, but generally are the responsi-
bility of all citizens.
Worldwide, an estimated 967 million daily smokers consumed ap-
proximately 6.25 trillion cigarettes over the course of 2012 (Ng et al.,
2014). According to the Tobacco Atlas (https://tobaccoatlas.org), a
project of the American CancerSociety andthe Vital Strategies,in 2016
the number of cigarettes smoked decreased to 5.7 trillion. Despite this,
the number is expected to reach nine trillion by 2025 due to further
growth in the market (Mackay et al., 2006). This is mainly due to po-
pulation growth (Srbinoska et al., 2018). The number of cigarettes per
smoker per day also varies widely across countries (Ng et al., 2014).
Fig. 1shows the global cigarette consumption per region between
1999 and 2016. The trend highlights the regions affected by higher
growth (China and South-East Asia Region - SEARO) and those char-
acterized by a decline (European Region -EURO, Americas - AMRO,
Western Pacific Region - WPRO - excluding China). This trend is re-
flected in the per capita consumption and on the waste associated with
cigarette consumption, as shown inFigs. 2 and 3.
CBs are the main type of litter: they are one of the most frequently
collected waste items during clean-ups, in particular in beach and
coastal environments (Araújoa and Costa, 2019;Ocean Conservancy,
2018). The estimated annually discarded waste from global cigarette
consumption could be anywhere between 340–680 million kg, besides
almost two million tonnes of paper, ink, cellophane, foil and glue that
are used in tobacco product packaging. More than 40% of all items
collected during urban clean-ups and removed from public areas such
as beaches and parks are CBs and related products (Bonanomi et al.,
2015;Arizaetal., 2008).Accordingto WHO (2017),uptotwo-thirdsof
every smoked cigarette is discarded on the ground, and about 76%
according to Patel et al. (2013). This situation is explained byPatel
et al. (2013)and Novotny and Zhao (1999): through interviews, they
found that 73.5% of smokers do not use correct practices for the dis-
posal of CBs, which are often therefore released into the environment.
According to Roder Green et al. (2014), CBs are not uniformly
distributed in an urban area, but are strongly connected to the location
of sales and consumption. Their research (concentrated in the US)
showed evidence of a higher concentration in proximity to bars, con-
venience stores, liquor stores, cafes, gas stations, grocery stores, res-
taurants, and traffic signals, as also reported byMarah and Novotny
(2011) and Van Gucht et al. (2010). Furthermore, the authors report
how CBs are concentrated at places of transition from an outdoor to an
indoor environment (entrances to buildings, vehicles or public trans-
portation stations).
The big problem associated with this waste, in addition to its lack of
control as it is freely dispersed in the environment, is that it is not
biodegradable and that it contains and releases over 7000 toxic che-
micals in the environment.
Its toxicity and non-biodegradability could justify environmental
regulation and policies to raise the price of tobacco and further dis-
courage its use (Smith and McDaniel, 2010).
However, practical operational aspects are lacking, in particular at
the regulatory level. For example, the European List of Waste (EC,
2000; EU, 2014) does not propose a specific entry for CBs, which is
thereforeconsideredas“Municipalwasteincludingseparatelycollected
fraction/Separately collected fraction/Other fractions not otherwise
specified” (Rebischung et al., 2018).
Therefore, using a "precautionary principle" and appropriate
awareness raising and accountability practices among consumers and
producers would substantially contribute to reducing the production of
waste and its dispersion into the environment.
Currently, given the poor results, finding effective management
solutions for this waste is essential as it has been widely demonstrated
how conventional disposal methods mainly represented by landfilling
or incineration are neither universally sustainable nor economically
feasible for this purpose (Bandi et al., 2018;Mohajerani et al., 2016).
Identifyingarecyclingmethodologyforthiskindofwastewouldreduce
the release of dangerous materials into the environment and to the
ecosystems, and would promote the recovery of materials in line with
the circular economy and sustainable development.

2. Characteristics of cigarette filters and CBs
A cigarette is a small cylinder of finely cut tobacco leaves rolled in
thin paper.
Modern commercially manufactured cigarettes are composed of the
following main elements (Fig. 4):
•Tobacco blend dried and cut into thin one millimeter thick strips.
The characteristics of tobacco change from manufacturer to manu-
facturer. Dangerous chemicals can form in and be deposited on to-
bacco during the processing of the tobacco leaves. Other dangerous
chemicals are created when the tobacco filler is burned. Often there
are also various additives that manufacturers can add to make
smoking more appealing and to mask the harshness of the smoke.
Humectants such as propylene glycol or glycerol, as well as fla-
vouring products and enhancers such as cocoa solids, liquorice, to-
bacco extracts, and various sugars are some of the additives in ci-
garettes. There are hundreds of possible additives, however these
are not declared on the cigarette packages on the market.
•Rolling paper to cover the tobacco and adhesive. Composed of cel-
lulose(withpossibleadditives),thispaperfavourstheappearanceof
the ash and guarantees a uniform combustion. It is closed by an
adhesive substance.
•Afiltermadeupofcelluloseacetate,describedinmoredetailbelow.
•Tipping paper to cover the filter. This is a polyvinyl acetate emul-
sion which is used to glue the cellulose acetate plug to the inner
wrapping paper.
According to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services
(USDHHS), smokers are exposed to a toxic mix of over 7000 chemicals
when they inhale cigarette smoke (U.S. Department of Health and
Human Services (USDHHS, 2010). Hundreds of these substances are
toxic, about 70 are carcinogenic.
In 2012 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) published a
preliminary list of 93 Harmful and Potentially Harmful Constituents
(HPHCs) that are linked to the five most serious health effects of to-
bacco use (cancer, cardiovascular disease, respiratory effects, re-
productive problems, and addiction). Some of these substances are

An important element that makes up the cigarette is the filter (CF).
CFs were introduced in the middle of the 20th century, based on a
patent by Boris Aivaz recorded in 1925.
A filter is designed to reduce the amount of smoke, tar and fine
particles inhaled during the combustion and to reduce the harshness of
the smoke and keep tobacco flakes out of the smoker's mouth. The filter
captures andretains toxic substances,butwhen littered, such chemicals
can leach out of the cigarette filter.
According toMohajerani et al. (2016), almost 90% of all cigarettes
are manufactured with cellulose acetate filter tips except in Japan,
Venezuela, South Korea and Hungary.
CFs are made of about 95% poorly degradable microscopic-sized
whitefibresmassedtogether(about12,000perfilterwithabout30,000
dernier), characterized by a Y-shaped cross-section that is not perpen-
dicular to the flow (the angle ranges from 18° to 49°) (Du et al., 2015)
and it is made of CA. These fibres are synthetic plastic which looks like
cotton. In the CFs, the CA fibres are linked to each other through the
glyceroltriacetate,whichisaplasticizer(HamzahandUmar, 2017).CA
is one of the most important esters of cellulose. It is produced through a
two-step process, involving the esterification of acetic acid with cellu-
lose and the partial hydrolysis of the resulting ester groups. CA can be
produced with varying degrees of substitution. The most common form
of cellulose acetate fibre has an acetate group on approximately 2–2.5
of every three hydroxyls (Fischer et al., 2008), due to good solubility in
common solvents, molecular weights, and melt properties (Puls et al.,
2011). CA has various applications which depend on its processing and
its physico-chemical characteristics. In particular, it is used for the
production of textiles, plastics, films, and CFs.
The common name for the remains of a cigarette after smoking is a
CB. According toPoppendieck et al. (2016), aCB can be defined as “the
cigarette remaining at the conclusion of the smouldering phase fol-
lowing smoking. The conclusion of the smouldering phase is defined as
when the entire cigarette butt reaches the ambient temperature of the
environment in which it is located. The CB includes any remaining
paper (wrapping paper, tipping paper, and plug wrap paper)”. It is ty-
pically about 30% of the cigarette's original length.
The three major CB components that Poppendieck et al. (2016)
listed are: 1) ash, 2) unburned tobacco and 3) filtration material
(Fig. 6).
A filter, after its use, has absorbed smoke, trap tar, toxic chemicals
and particle smoke. Its chemical composition depends on the type of
tobacco used, the cigarette paper, category and effectiveness of the
filteraswell asthedegreeoftipventilation.Italsodependsonthefilter
design. Strain et al. (2015)analyzed the smoke filtering properties of
different fibres using a cigarette as the smoke source. The tests showed
thepresence ofmanycarcinogenichydrocarbonsandalcoholsthatexist
in tobacco smoke. Experiments have also shown that the original CA
cigarette filter tip absorbed only 2.7 times its own weight during the
same treatment.
ThemainproblemassociatedwithCBsistheirslowdegradationrate
and the high concentration of toxic content.
Typically, cellulose acetate possesses poor photo- and bio- degrad-
able properties, making CBs persistent in the environment. The
methods and times required for this process depend on the degradation
mechanism, of which there are different types, e.g. photodegradation,
biodegradation, and mechanical processes or a combination of these as
typically occurs in outdoor environments due to different factors. Some
researchers have demonstrated how the speed of degradation processes
are inversely proportional to time. In particular, Puls et al. (2011)
carried out a review of degradation mechanisms and the potential de-
gradation rates of CA. According toPuls et al. (2011), cellulose is de-
gradable thanks to organisms that utilize cellulose enzymes. CA, due to
the additional acetyl groups, requires the presence of esterases for the
first step in degradation. Also, the high molecular weight, crystallinity
and the physical form influence the microbial degradation efficacy
(Haske-Cornelius et al., 2017;Ogundare et al., 2017). Furthermore, CA
isphoto-chemicallydegradedbyUV,butitspropertyislimitedtophoto
degradability in sunlight due to the lack of chromophores for absorbing
ultraviolet light (Robertson et al., 2012). Thus cellulose acetate-based
cigarette filters do not biodegrade under most circumstances because of
their compressed make upand the presence of acetyl molecules (Haske-
Cornelius et al., 2017).
CBs require several years to degrade completely (Bonanomi et al.,
2015;Puls et al., 2011). According toKadir et al. (2009), the degrading
rates vary from within 1 to 2 months under anaerobic conditions, 6 to 9
months in soil, 12 months in fresh water and 36 months or more under
poor conditions, for example, sea water, desert and underground sta-
tions. Bonanomi et al. (2015)has demonstrated this slow process of
degradation through a 2-year experiment in a standard decomposing
organic substrate. The study shows how CBs lost only 37.8% of their
initial mass after two years of decomposition. Comparing cellulose and
plastic cigarette filter decomposition,Joly and Coulis (2018)estimated
that conventional plastic filters take 7.5–14 years to disappear in
compost and on the soil surface, respectively. In contrast, they esti-
mated that cellulose filters take 2.3–13 years to disappear, in compost
and on the soil surface, respectively.
The other significant problem is the over 7000 toxic chemicals
(including up to 50 human carcinogens according toHoffmann and
Hoffmann (1997), toxic heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydro-
carbons, pesticides, N-nitrosamines, aromatic amines, formaldehyde,
acetaldehyde, benzene and nicotine) that this waste releases into the
water, the soil, and the air, and which then comes into direct contact
with humans, animals and vegetation.
Poppendieck et al. (2016)provided a very in-depth review of the
chemicals in cigarette butt emission and extraction studies, divided by
media (air, water, extract solution, cigarette filter, cigarette butt),
which summarized the chemicals found in the environment are a po-
tential threaten to human health and the environment, especially
marine ecosystems. These problems, in particular associated with
harmful chemicals, have been analysed by several other authors.
Roder Green et al. (2014)studied the effects of the nicotine con-
tained in CBs on urban water resources through batch and rainfall ex-
periments. Slaughter et al. (2011)analysed the effects on fish exposed
to CBs soaked in either fresh or salt water.Singh and Kathiresan (2015)
analysed the effects on fish and microbial communities.Oropesa et al.
(2017) studied the effects on aquatic organisms from different trophic
levels. This process revealed that nicotine poses an important risk to
aquatic organisms.Booth et al. (2015)tested how common intertidal
molluscs were affected by butt leachate.
Slaughter et al. (2011)showed that percolates from CBs are toxic to
a range of marine organisms. The LC50 (lethal concentration capable of
killing 50% of the animals used in the test) is 1 CB/l for both marine
and freshwater species.

3. Methodology
This paper reviews the recent studies on CB recycling solutions. The
scientific literature contains many interesting examples; however, the
present review focuses only on specific experiences of CBs, which used
this waste as input for physical and/or chemical treatment in order to
obtain second raw materials, semi-finished products and finished goods
applicable in industrial, agricultural, civil engineering, environmental
or chemical contexts.
The research was conducted on papers published prior to 1 January
2019 using ScienceDirect and Scopus databases. The keywords used
were: “Cigarette Butt”, “Cigarette Filter” and “Cigarette Tip”. These
keywords were searched for individually or coupled with the term
"recycling" using the "*" wildcard character used for multi-word sear-
ches. The results of the search, expressed as the number of papers
identified, are summarized inTable 1.
Despite the numerous papers available, most were excluded from
this work as they were not related to the topic discussed in this docu-
ment. In fact, CBs are the subject of numerous related studies, for ex-
ample, assessing environmental impacts and ecotoxicity, pollutant
emission rates, physical dispersion, health effects, etc.
The papers identified were further filtered by selecting only those
conforming to the focus of the present study: articles that directly stu-
died possible ways for the recovery and recycling of CBs.
Atotalof37paperswerethusselectedwhichconsiderthedefinition
of CBs, their chemical composition, techniques to treat and purify the
smoked CBs, the products and materials that can be obtained and the
performances of the products obtained.
The papers selected were classified using criteria that describe the
process input, sample treatment system, output and possible use. The
geographical distribution of the studies was also considered. The five
criteria used were (Fig. 7):
1 Input for recycling: identifies the types of samples used as input to
the treatment process. CBs are differentiated according to the filter
production and characteristics of the smoked tobacco. Typically,
this type of study compares CBs smoked with new and unused CFs
(Table 2). Few authors used filters smoked in the laboratory to
mimic the smoking process.
2 Methodology/Treatment system: identifies the type of approach
adopted for the treatment of samples, identifying methods of che-
mical treatment, physical treatment and physical-chemical treat-
ment. A brief description is also provided aimed at highlighting the
methodological steps used by the authors (Table 3).
3 Type of product obtained from the process: characterizes the type of
product obtained from the treatment process, identifying its phy-
sical state be it a solid, liquid or gaseous product. This criterion
characterizes the output of the treatment process (Table 4).
4 Possible uses: describes the results of the process and describes its
possible use. This criterion characterizes the possible use of the
output obtained (Table 5).
5 Geographical distribution: identifies the country where the study
was conducted (Table 6).
The results obtained from the evaluation of each criterion are de-
scribed in the Section4.
4. Results
Considering the final objective of the present study and assessing
the results of recent studies on CB recycling solutions, the outcomes of
the review are presented by individually analysing the criteria identi-
fied and described in Section4.
4.1. Sample input
The reviewed papers are classified inTable 2according to the type
of CBs used as input to the treatment and recycling process.
Theanalysedpapersapplyapproachesandtechniquesusingsmoked
or unsmoked filters. The use of one or the other, or a comparison of the
performance of the type of input used, depends on the authors’ meth-
odological approach.
Of the 37 selected contributions, most of the authors (about 97%)
usedsmokedCBstodevelopandtestrecyclingsolutions.Onlytwocases
(about 3%) used mimicked smoked CBs (one author) or supplementary
materials (one author) as a precursor to the treatment and recovery
process. Only five papers (about 14%) proposed a comparison using
unused CFs. This is important because it checks whether and how the
recycling process could influence the results, comparing the use of fil-
ters to "pure" material (cellulose acetate).
This comparison was conducted by Blankenship and Mokaya
(2017), who used cigarette filters / butts to form the precursor for
porous carbons in order to evaluate their hydrogen storage capacity.
The results showed that a sequential benign hydrothermal carbonisa-
tion and activation treatment obtained a super-porous and ultra-high
surface area (4300m2 g−1) and pore volume (2.09cm3g−1) arising
almost entirely (>90%) from micropores. These characteristics, com-
bined with the oxygen-rich nature of the material, demonstrated the
highest hydrogen storage capacity reported in all the papers analysed.
Thecomparisonbetweensmokedandunusedfiltershighlightedvarious
anomalies of the smoked samples mainly due to the presence of heavy
metals.
Polarz et al. (2002)studied the transformation of cellulose acetate
filters into micro-meter or nano-meter porous carbon structures. Ap-
plying an easy direct process, filters from smoked or unsmoked cigar-
ettes were transformed into non-graphitic carbon (defined by the In-
ternational Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry as “all varieties of
solids consisting mainly of the element carbon with two-dimensional long-
range order of the carbon atoms in planar hexagonal networks, but without
any measurable crystallographic order in the third direction (c-direction)
apart from more or less parallel stacking” and with many concrete ap-
plications such as heat shields, Li-ion batteries, activated carbons). The
resulting material exhibited a well-defined mesoporosity, with different
characteristics from the cellulose acetate fibres used as a precursor and
withagoodpotentialapplicationinthefield ofgaspurificationor more
generally as filters.
Gómez Escobar and Maderuelo Sanz (2017)analyzed the perfor-
mance of smoked and unused CBs as absorbing materials. Each sample
was obtained by collecting 10 butts inside an impedance tube. The
samples differed in the type of butt used and their physical character-
istics (length and diameter). All showed a high sound absorption

The solution of using by-products resulting from the treatment
processes of CBs in the construction industry and infrastructure has the
greatest support among the authors analysed, with 31% of studies de-
scribing this possible use.
This research area has been extensively studied byMohajerani et al.
(2016); Kadir and Mohajerani (2015);Sarani and Kadir (2013);Kadir
and Mohajerani (2011);Kadir et al. (2009)and Kadir and Mohajerani
(2008) and reviewed byBories et al. (2014).
They evaluated the use of CBs as a mixed component in the pre-
paration of fired bricks, which is considered as common solution to
pollution and is already applied with other waste (e.g. rubber, lime-
stone dust and wood sawdust, processed waste tea, fly ash, polystyrene,
and sludge).
These authors demonstrated that adding different concentrations of
CBs to the compound (from 0% to 10% by weight and corresponding to
about 0–30% by volume) for the production of fired bricks, leads to a
reduction in the density of the fired bricks, an increase in porosity, and
a decrease in thermal conductivity. Low-density or light-weight bricks
have several advantages over standard bricks (lower structural dead
load, easier handling, lower transport costs, different thermal con-
ductivity), except when stronger bricks are needed. However, this
method also leads to a reduction in compressive strength (although in
the case studies, the results were lower than the acceptable regulatory
Australian limits) which is also important when considering their use.
With controlled temperatures, it is also possible to reduce the energy
usage during firing and gas emissions into the atmosphere. The related
atmospheric emissions have been studied in detail through experi-
mental tests that compared the Estimated Total Emission (ETE) of dif-
ferent types of bricks produced at different heating rates during firing
(Mohajerani et al., 2016and Kadir and Mohajerani, 2015).
These results demonstrate how CBs can be considered as a potential
addition to raw materials used in the manufacturing of light fired
bricks.
Another use analyzed by the authors is related to the production of
asphalt for streets and as prefabricated concrete paving.Mohajerani
et al. (2017)studied the possibility of incorporating CBs into asphalt
concrete for pavement construction, encapsulating CBs with different
classes of bitumen and paraffin wax. Encapsulation prevents chemical
translocation from CBs to the environment. Comparing the results ob-
tained in the two cases with the control asphalt concrete sample (no
CBs), using mechanical and volumetric properties, including stability,
flow, resilient modulus, bulk density, maximum density, air voids, and
voids in mineral aggregates as variables, the proposed solutions were
found to satisfy the requirements for light, medium and heavy traffic
conditions for bitumen samplers, while paraffin wax only satisfied the
light traffic conditions for road pavements.
The authors identified this solution as a useful contribution to the
decrease in urban heat effect in urban environments due to the reduc-
tion in bulk density, the increase in porosity and the reduction of
thermal conductivity
A similar study was also carried out by Cui et al. (2018) and
Wadalkar et al. (2018). They analyzed the possible benefits of in-
corporating CBs into precast concrete blocks for paving, assessing their
performance as the percentage of added CB increases. They observed
that the compressive strength decreased with an increase in the pro-
portion of CBs, while flexural strength and water absorption increased.
To exploit the sound insulation capabilities of CBs,Maderuelo-Sanz
et al. (2018)and Gómez Escobar and Maderuelo Sanz (2017)demon-
strated the high sound absorption performance of this material and the
potential use of CBs as a component for insulating solutions, which
were found to be highly competitive even with commercial solutions.
In the field of environmental engineering, the products deriving
fromtherecyclingofCBsareaboveallappliedinthetreatmentofwater

applicable to accidental conditions, with the release of oil, methylene
blue (MB) or liquid petroleum derivatives. Among the various solutions
(natural, chemical, and mechanical remediation), the use of oil-absor-
bent materials is one of the most economical and effective approaches,
due to the low operational costs, easy operation and high efficiency.

The use of CBs for energy purposes has also proved to be of interest
in the literature. A total of 19% of the authors analyzed reported re-
search on energy, particularly in the field of new, clean, and high-ef-
ficiency energy storage devices.
Xiong et al. (2018b)studied the use of CBs as a precursor for the
development of supercapacitors, which are one of the best potential
energy storage systems thanks to higher energy and power densities
than conventional capacitors. They developed a new application of CBs
for energy storage, producing carbon with a large specific capacitance
and good capacitance retention through the combination of CBs and
polypyrrole (PPy). PPy is a conducting polymer used as an electrode
material for supercapacitors. The excellent storage performance ob-
tained by the authors was due to the synergistic effects of the porous
backbone structure derived from CBs and the pseudocapacitance be-
havior of PPy-derived nitrogen functionality.
Lee et al. (2014)also developed a supercapacitor based on CBs as
the active material, producing a nitrogen doped (N-doped) meso-/mi-
croporous hybrid carbon material (NCF), which is a porous carbon
material that spontaneously contains both mesopores and micropores.
The results highlighted a good capability and higher specific capaci-
tance during 6000 charge and discharge cycles.
In relation to hydrogen, as an attractive energy source especially for
motor vehicles, Blankenship and Mokaya (2017) demonstrated that
porous carbons derived from CBs with a super porous, ultra-high sur-
face area and pore volume can reach for storing hydrogen. The com-
bination of high surface area, high microporosity and the oxygen-rich
nature of the carbons material, ensures a storage capacity of 8.1wt%
excess uptake, and 9.4wt% total uptake at −196°C and 20bar, rising
to a total uptake of 10.4wt% and 11.2wt% at 30 and 40bar, respec-
tively.
Kim et al. (2016)developed highly efficient catalysts for the oxygen
reduction reaction based on CBs. This is a bimodal porous nitrogen (N)
doped carbon supported Pt composite for the industrial development of
fuel cells.
Ghosh et al. (2017) showed how pyrolysis treatment produces a
material with good conductive properties which can be further used in
various electronic fields.
Wang et al. (2016)used waste metal ions and CBs at the same time
to develop a hybrid electrode material of carbon fiber and vanadium
nitride (CF@VN) with a hierarchical porous structure. This was used as
a negative electrode to develop an asymmetric supercapacitor, using an
activated carbon as a positive electrode. The resulting product showed
good electro-chemical behavior.

5. Conclusions and prospects for development
Despitecigarettebutts(CBs)being oneof themost commontypesof
litter worldwide,with a production of one million tonnes eachyear, the
solution to the problem still appears very challenging and distant.
In fact, this involves at least two main aspects:
1 Consumers: increasing awareness of the environmental impact of
CBs and changing their habits in dealing with this waste (today in
most cases still discarded on the ground). Policies and awareness
campaigns are used to achieve this.
2 Management methodologies, identifying one (or more) economical
andeffective technologicalsolutions for the prevention or treatment
of this waste and also identifying the best approaches for the col-
lection and transport of CBs.
This paper has analysed the second aspect through a general review
of 37 selected papers that provide different types of solutions for the
treatment and recycling of CBs.
The following conclusions can be drawn from the results presented
in this paper:
•CBs constitute widespread waste globally and are often more con-
centrated in areas with the highest number of consumers. China is
anexampleofthissituation:peoplesmokeover40%ofallcigarettes
globally (2043 cigarettes per person per year) and has the largest
number of CBs and packaging waste in the world.
•This aspect also influences the origin of the studies analysed, which
are often concentrated in areas where the problem appears to be
very significant or, as in the case of India and Mexico, where local
conditions require dedicated solutions (e.g. integrated vector man-
agement strategies).
•Comparing the authors’ approaches to the treatment of CBs, in most
tests only smoked CBs were used, often without providing com-
parative analyses of the same non-smoked material (CFs cleaned).
•Thepreparationandtreatmentmethodsofthesamplesaremainlyof
a physical nature, through which the CBs are separated from the
remaining non-smoked tobacco, and from the outer paper and cru-
shed into small fragments.
•The results of the treatment processes constitute in particular solid
materials used in numerous economic and productive sectors,
especially in the field of building materials, energy and the en-
vironment and chemistry.
•Few authors describe the methods of collecting the CB samples. In
fact, laboratory applications do not require large quantities of
samples. However, the logistical aspect associated with the collec-
tion and handling of CBs is an extremely critical element of the
process, which risks making the product developed ineffective and
the economic system not economically viable.
•The treatment processes described all take place on a laboratory
pilot scale. However, a large-scale analysis or experimental appli-
cationislacking,whichwouldsimulatetheoperationalprocesswith
greater quantities and with the critical, operational, economic and
legislative barriers that characterize a pilot case.
•With the exception of very few studies, most are very recent scien-
tific contributions. This shows how the correct management of CBs
represents a current priority of global interest.
•All the studies analysed describe the methodological and operative
treatments of CBs and analyse the results and the products obtained
in detail. This provides very detailed information on the character-
istics of the resulting materials and performance under operating
conditions. However, no author provides detailed information re-
garding the economic sustainability of the process or how to make
these products suitable for the market.
•Finally, the review highlights that social aspects are still poorly in-
vestigated and, in most cases, neglected. Including social factors

Despite the interesting and promising results presented in the lit-
erature, CBs remain a particularly critical waste due to their char-
acteristics and impact on the environment and on the health of living
organisms. Therefore, the main challenge of preventing the production
and spread of this waste remains.
Avoiding the production of waste according to the "precautionary
principle" remains the optimal solution that must be increasingly ap-
plied and disseminated (WHO, 2017). The application of this principle
is not simple, therefore solutions are also needed to avoid dispersion,
that would facilitate the correct collection and management of CBs.
Below are some examples of possible measures to reduce the pro-
duction and dispersion of CBs in the environment:
•Labeling the danger. Just as health risks are reported on packets,
this information could also state that CBs and packaging products
are a non-biodegradable hazardous waste. A description of the main
effects on the environment could be included together with some
recommendations on the correct disposal methods to reduce their
dispersion.
•Using biodegradable materials and banning disposable filters. The
use of biodegradable materials for the production of CFs and
packaging products could be encouraged. There are already various
international initiatives that reduce the impact and increase the
sustainability of the box, inner liner, ink, filter, tobacco and cigar-
ette paper.
•A "Deposit / Return" approach. This approach has already been
successfully applied to other materials, for example glass bottles.
The same approach, which includes a deposit for the purchase,
could also be applied to the sale of cigarette packets. The “butt
deposit” could be refunded when the packet is returned to the
vender with the butts.
•A waste tax. A specific tax could be introduced that is added to the
cost of cigarettes and used to finance specific activities, such as
cleaning up local areas, financing research projects, and so on.
•Anti-smoking policies and waste fines. This solution requires poli-
ticalinterventionbylocaladministrationsthatcouldbansmokingin
certain critical areas. The penalty for offenders would be a fine. This
solution has already been tested: for example in 2019 several Italian
beaches have been smoke-free. This prevents the presence and ac-
cumulation of CBs in critical areas, such as coastal areas.
•Litigation against the tobacco industry. This solution requires spe-
cific even economic interventions of the tobacco industries to pro-
mote solutions to the production and dispersion of CBs in the en-
vironment. Often, expensive legal battles are needed to obtain the
support of the industry.
•CB collection campaigns. This solution is already internationally
widespread with teams of volunteers involved in waste collection
(including CBs) in some areas (beaches in particular). Also thanks to
