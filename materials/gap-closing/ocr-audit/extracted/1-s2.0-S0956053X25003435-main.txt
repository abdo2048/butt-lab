ABSTRACT
Healthcare Waste Management (HCWM) contributes to environmental pollution and health hazards. There are 
several established approaches to measure environmental and health impacts from HCWM. Amidst, Life Cycle 
Assessment (LCA) is one of the most popular tools to evaluate such impacts. This research was conducted to 
compare environmental impacts, and Microplastics (MPs) and Heavy Metals (HMs) emissions through LCA from 
HCWM between public and private Healthcare Facilities (HCFs) in Chattogram, Bangladesh. Five case studies were 
conducted based on the size of HCFs including one ideal scenario (developed with minimum impacts). The LCA 
was evaluated by SimaPro version 9.5.0.0 software and the ReCiPe 2016 method. Results revealed that private 
HCFs highly contributed to the eighteen studied impact categories. The main impact categories were human 
toxicity, global warming, ecotoxicity, eutrophication, acidification and resource scarcity. The existing common 
Healthcare Waste (HCW) disposal options were waste transport, open burning, recycling and dumping. The ranges 
of major impacts from HCWM were global warming (0.588–1.122 kg CO2 eq), followed by human non- 
carcinogenic toxicity (0.002–0.003 kg CO2 eq.) and ecotoxicity (0.000–0.001 kg CO2 eq.). It demonstrated 
that dumping of HCW, landfill leachate, open burning/ incineration ash and recycling processes along with 
wastewater of recycling plants are the potential sources of HMs (up to 14480 mg/kg waste) and MPs (1906–7223 
items/kg waste) pollution. This study demonstrates the potential levels of environmental impacts along with 
HMs and MPs emissions from the HCWM system, which will facilitate the relevant authorities and policy makers 
to adopt the required strategies.
1. Introduction
Healthcare Waste (HCW) is a broad topic that covers all sorts of solid 
wastes generated from Healthcare Facilities (HCFs) such as hospitals, 
clinics, diagnostic centers, labs, veterinary clinics, research centers and 
nursing homes (Fan et al., 2022; Hossain and Alam, 2012, Janik- 
Karpinska et al., 2023). HCW is also known as medical, hospital, clin-
ical and biomedical waste. It contains infectious agents such as dis-
carded cultures, stocks, specimens, tissue, blood, body fluids, sharps, 
Hepatitis B virus, Hepatitis C virus and HIV. This type of wastes is 
ranging from 10 % to 15 % of the total waste; therefore, HCWs are called 
dangerous/hazardous/biohazardous waste (Alam et al., 2015; Barua 
et al., 2015; Hossain and Alam, 2013). The percentage of infectious 
waste varied in HCW based on sources and regions. Mid December 2019, 
COVID-19 pandemic broke out in Wuhan, China and rapidly spread out 
throughout the world in 2020. This virus can transmit from person to 
person through direct physical contact, air and other potential ways 
(Belhadi et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 2021; Nabavi-Pelesaraei et al., 2022). 
Coronavirus is contagious and very dangerous, so people started to wear 
several Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) such as aprons, facemasks, 
goggles and hand gloves. Most of these PPEs, especially single use 
facemasks are globally used in large quantities and then discarded (Ajaj 
et al., 2023; Kokalj et al., 2022; Snigdha et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2022). 
Such emerging wastes are responsible for booming the volume of total 
HCW. Therefore, it is a very challenging task to safely handle and 
dispose of such continuously growing waste piles (Friedericy et al.,

2022; Kumar et al., 2021; Türkmen, 2022). Mishandling of HCW is 
responsible for spreading infectious agents (HIV/AIDS, COVID-19, 
Hepatitis B and C viruses) and unwanted accidents from sharp mate-
rials (Alam et al., 2014; Ghodrat et al., 2017; Hossain et al., 2013). 
Therefore, the HCW generation rate and its composition is very impor-
tant for safe disposal.
Generally, HCW generation rate varies based on sources and regions. 
Previous studies demonstrated HCW generation rate 0.36–0.67 kg/cap/ 
day and 1.3–3.0 kg/bed/day in developed countries while 2.17–2.63 
kg/cap/day and 1.50–3.10 kg/bed/day in developing countries (Alam 
et al., 2013; Deepak et al., 2022; Hossain and Alam, 2012; Zhou et al., 
2024). A major fraction of HCW is food waste in developing countries; 
however, it contains a significant amount of plastics & rubber, metals, 
glass, and small metallic tools. HCW consists of 6 %–20 % Solid Plastic 
Waste (SPW). Similarly, a large part of PPE is made of polymers. 
Therefore, the percentage of SPW greatly increased in HCW since 
COVID-19 out-break (Klemes et al., 2020; Kokalj et al., 2022; Liu et al., 
2022; Rehman et al., 2025; Stapleton et al., 2023). These SPWs gradu-
ally degrade during recycling, treatment and disposal of HCW. Thus, 
macroplastics (<10 mm), Microplastics (MPs) (<5 mm) and nanoplastics 
(<1 μm) are generated from SPW throughout its life cycle (Alam et al., 
2018; Ajaj et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2021; Zhao and You, 2022, 2023). 
With the passage of time, these MPs are distributed to urban environ-
ments and adjacent water bodies which subsequently reach to seas and 
oceans (Alam et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2023; Gallo et al., 2018; Liu 
et al., 2020; Silva et al., 2021). MPs are ingested by marine fish and then 
enter food chains. Finally, MPs are digested by human beings through 
food chains (Suzuki et al., 2022; Woods et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; 
Zhao and You, 2022; Zhong et al., 2024b).
Similarly, non-organic HCW wastes contain Heavy Metals (HMs) 
which are blended during their manufacture (Alam et al., 2018; Alam 
and Qiao, 2020; Alam et al., 2024b; Zahoor et al., 2023). The great 
challenge of HCW incineration is the emission of toxic HMs (e.g., Cr, Cd, 
Pb and Hg), dioxins, furans, and Persistent Organic Pollutants (POP). 
Incineration cannot destroy metallic components of HCW, so HMs are 
concentrated in the bottom ash (Adama et al., 2016; Alam et al., 2024a; 
Zhao et al., 2009; Zheng et al., 2024b). Besides, illegal dumping and 
landfilling release HMs to soil that subsequently reach adjacent water 
bodies and groundwater. Therefore, HCW disposal is a major environ-
mental and health concern (Alam et al., 2014; Voudrias, 2023; Zheng 
et al., 2024a). Recently, management of landfill leachate and incinera-
tion ash has been under continuous scrutiny and control due to such 
concern (Adama et al., 2016; Liu and Terumasa, 2022; Zhong et al., 
2024a).
Though COVID-19 wastes and general HCW are separately and safely 
handled and disposed in developed countries; however, it is often mixed 
up with Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) in developing countries (Ahmad 
et al., 2021; Alam and Qiao, 2020; Dey and Ray, 2023; Zhao et al., 
2021). Therefore, it is a timely demand to develop a cost-effective and 
eco-friendly strategy for HCW disposal in developing countries focusing 
local scenarios. The application of Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) tool can 
support in this case for developing waste management strategy. LCA 
based studies were commenced in 1969 and developed in the 1980s. 
Recently, LCA is considered as the most popular tool to evaluate the 
environmental footprints and cost analysis of MSW management and 
disposal (Cavalloro et al., 2024; Dey and Ray, 2020, 2021; Maria et al., 
2021; Mu˜noz et al., 2018; Piekarska et al., 2023; Sonibare et al., 2019; 
Song et al., 2017). However, it was first applied in HCW in 2006 by Qing 
et al. (2006). The application of LCA in HCW is still limited in developed 
countries like the U.S., the U.K., China, Brazil and E.U. countries 
(Nabavi-Pelesaraei et al., 2022; Soares et al., 2013; Thiel et al., 2015; 
Zhao et al., 2009) and very rare in developing countries (Alam and 
Mosharraf, 2019; Ali et al., 2016; Kumar et al., 2021).
Till now, several LCA studies were conducted to measure environ-
mental impacts of Healthcare Waste Management (HCWM), and recently 
added facemasks disposal (Ajaj et al., 2023; Deepak et al., 2022; 
Türkmen, 2022; Zhao et al., 2021). Several LCA studies were performed 
comparing incineration and non-incineration treatment of HCW (Ahmad 
et al., 2019; Diaz et al., 2005; Ghodrat et al., 2017; Qing et al., 2006; 
Zhao et al., 2009), pyrolysis treatment (Li et al., 2022; Yousef et al., 
2022), disposable medical facemasks (Kokalj et al., 2022; Türkmen, 
2022; Yin et al., 2023), life cycle costing and economic assessment (Atia 
et al., 2020; Hong et al., 2018), chemical disinfection (Soares et al., 
2013), recycling (Liu et al., 2022; Shan et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023), 
reusable plastics/sharps, disposable materials and surgical instruments 
(Baruffaldi et al., 2019; Campion et al., 2015; Friedericy et al., 2022; 
Leiden et al., 2020; McPherson et al., 2019; Snigdha et al., 2023), lab-
oratory/ pathological test (Gordon et al., 2021; Ji et al., 2022; McAlister 
et al., 2021,2022), wastewater treatment (Lutterbeck et al., 2020; Wang 
et al., 2020) and resources consumptions in HCFs (Twomey, 2011). 
What are the environmental emissions from HCWM based on the size of 
HCFs and operational functions along with focusing on HMs and MPs 
emissions during HCW disposal and treatment is still unexplored?
According to literature, there is no published research report 
focusing on LCA of cumulative disinfection, recycling, illegal disposal 
(open burning and dumping), treatment and sanitary landfilling of HCW 
(Alam and Mosharraf, 2019; Alam and Qiao, 2020; Chen et al., 2014). 
LCA study in HCWM is a challenging task due to the absence of a uni-
form management system and database based on local scenarios, espe-
cially in developing countries. Therefore, it is a timely demand to 
conduct a study on HCWM to explore the differences between public and 
private HCFs in terms of environmental footprints from HCW disposal 
system. This LCA study was conducted to explore the HCWM system 
with minimum environmental impacts including potential MPs and HMs 
emissions.
The findings of this research will facilitate in identifying HCF (spe-
cifically based on public and private along with size) with the least 
environmental footprints from waste disposal options. Besides, the HMs 
and MPs emissions scenarios from different HCFs will support the 
management authorities and relevant policy makers to adopt the 
required strategies so that environmental emissions can be minimized.
2. Methodology
2.1. Background information
2.1.1. Primary data collection
The entire Chattogram City Corporation (CCC), Bangladesh was 
considered as a study area. It is situated within 22◦–14′ and 22◦–24′–30″ 
N Latitude and between 91◦–46′ and 91◦–53′ E Longitude. The city is 
located on the North bank of the river Karnafully. Two categories of 
respondents were selected with a view to collecting data such as HCW 
generator i.e. HCFs and HCW management authorities i.e. CCC (public) 
and Innovation Sheva Sangstha (ISS) (private). HCW generator re-
spondents were selected based on their importance and size, and the 
quantity of HCW they generate. The existing HCFs of CCC were classified 
into four categories based on their size and operating functions 
including (i) large scale hospital, (ii) medium scale clinic, (iii) small 
scale clinic/ diagnostic center, and (iv) rural clinic/ diagnostic center. 
The data were collected from selected HCFs namely Chattogram Medical 
College and Hospital (CMCH), University of Science and Technology, 
Chattogram (USTC), Memon Maternity Hospital (MMH), National Hospital 
(NH), City Corporation Charitable Clinic (CCCC), Metro Diagnostic Center 
(MDC), Patiya Upazila Health Complex (PUHC), and Chowdhury Hat 
Nursing Home (CHNH) because they can represent all the available HCFs 
in Chattogram. There are many differences between public and private 
HCFs in terms of operating functions and providing treatment services. 
Public HCFs have more functions and provide services to more patients 
than private one.
Two sets of semi-structured questionnaires were designed – one for 
the HCW generator and the other for the HCW management authorities 
were designed based on gathered experiences from empirical field visit

2.1.2. Healthcare waste management system
HCW solely contributes 2 %–3% to the daily generated MSWs vol-
ume in Chattogram City, Bangladesh. However, there is no similarity in 
HCW generation, handling, management and disposal of different cat-
egories of HCFs (public and private). Furthermore, there is absence of 
proper record keeping of resources consumption and daily HCW gen-
eration. The details about the existing HCWM system are shown in 
Fig. SI-1 (Supporting information). Bangladesh government has formu-
lated several rules for medical waste, hazardous materials, e-waste, solid 
waste and environment conservation such as (i) Medical Waste (Man-
agement and Processing) Rules, 2008, (ii) Solid Waste Management 
Rules 2021, and (iii) Environment Conservation Rules 2023 under the 
Bangladesh Environmental Protection Act, 1995. Following these reg-
ulations, public HCFs are practicing color coding bins but not properly 
followed in private HCFs. Therefore, most of the HCFs mix up the daily 
generated HCW that convert non-infectious waste into infectious. From 
sources, about 30 % HCW is collected by private organizations, while 35 
% by Community Based Organizations (CBOs) and 25 % by HCFs 
directly discharge to municipal dustbins. Besides, about 10 %–15 % 
HCW are illegally disposed of on roads, drains and fallow lands (Alam 
et al., 2014, 2015; Hossain and Alam, 2012; Hossain et al., 2013). 
Reusable metallic instruments are reused through steam/microwave 
sterilization and chemical disinfection. HCW are manually handled and 
segregated at both sources and dumpsite without wearing any protective 
gears. In addition, hardly sharp and infectious HCW are disposed of by 
bucket type incinerators without energy recovery. From the HCFs about 
40 %–55 % recyclable HCW such as plastics, papers, metals and glass 
directly go to scrap shops. Similarly, about 20 %–25 % HCW are 
disposed of through open burning while 10 %–20 % are buried (Alam 
and Hossain, 2013; Alam and Hossain, 2015; Hossain and Alam, 2012). 
The rest HCWs are transported to the dumpsite and disposed of through 
open burning and crude dumping. Again, the remaining recyclable HCW 
are picked up by scavengers from the dumpsite and sold to recycling 
shops. However, the HCWM services of both municipal and private or-
ganizations are not at satisfactory level due to the absence of energy 
recovery, leachate collection and gaseous emissions control system at 
disposal site. Therefore, the existing HCWM is polluting surrounding air, 
water and soil environments. However, the demand for private initiative 
services and adopting modern HCW treatment technologies are 
increasing because the municipality is not capable to handle HCW 
separately and control continuously increasing environmental pollution.
2.2. Life cycle assessment
The environmental and health impacts from HCW management and 
their treatment and disposal were evaluated using LCA according to the 
guidelines of “ISO 14040: Principles and Framework” and “ISO 14044: 
Requirements and Guidelines” (Ji et al., 2022; Leiden et al., 2020; Yin 
et al., 2023). This study adopted four steps: (i) goal and scope definition; 
(ii) development of system boundary, (iii) inventory data collection, (iv) 
impact assessment, (v) interpretation.
2.2.1. Goal and scope definition
This study aimed to compare the HCWM system between public and 
private HCFs based on the above mentioned four categories along with 
the proposed ideal one (with minimum environmental impacts), in 
terms of environmental footprints, emphasizing MPs and HMs pollution. 
This research covered all types of solid HCW transport, disinfection, 
recycling, open burning, incineration, crude dumping, burial, com-
posting and sanitary landfill. However, the management of wastewater 
from HCFs and life cycle cost are excluded because these data are not 
available in their records.

2.2.2. Development of system boundary
This study used a descriptive LCA framework to represent the HCWM 
system of Chattogram City including four case studies focusing the 
available management system based on their categories and one pro-
posed system (see Fig. 1). These case studies represent the different 
disposal and treatment options of the entire HCWM generated from the 
selected HCFs. They also covered the HCW generation stage to final 
disposal point. Here, HCW is mostly segregated manually without 
wearing any protective gears that is a potential source of health hazards 
of the waste handlers. Besides, open burning and dumping with MSW 
are two commonly practiced HCW disposal options. The readily recy-
clable wastes (plastics, metals and glass) are informally recycled without 
pretreatment that is silently killing the health of the associated workers. 
In addition, the percentage of different categories of HCW disposal 
through open burning, dumping, composting, recycling, treatment and 
landfilling are included in these case studies and the developed ideal 
scenario. Fig. 1 (CMCH) shows the percentage of total HCW disposed of 
by different disposal options. Notably, the same amount of disinfected 
HCW is reused or recycled (ideal scenario), so it showed repeated per-
centage. Then the actual quantity of each physical composition disposed 
of by each disposal option is displayed. Similarly, the other HCFs sce-
narios are presented.
Here, the ideal HCWM system was proposed considering waste 
disposal options with minimum environmental impacts. The ideal sys-
tem demonstrated comparatively lower environmental emissions than 
the existing HCWM systems. From Table 1, the numerical data of each 
physical composition was further calculated based on disposal options.

System boundary of any study depends on the goal and objectives of 
the study. The system boundary of this study is cradle to grave process. 
This study is confined within the activities of HCW management, 
treatment and disposal in HCFs. The impact of wastewater and its 
treatment is not considered here due to the unavailability of related 
data. The input in HCWM included solid waste, water, electricity, diesel 
and chemicals. The key output from the HCWM is the emissions to air, 
soil, wastewater/ leachate and solid waste (see Fig. 2). Open firing of 
HCW is arranged both in HCFs on-campus and dumping station for 
reducing wastes volume, that is liable for GHGs, PMs, VOCs, CO, NOx, 
SO2 and dioxin/furans emissions to the atmosphere. The illegal dumping 
and landfilling of HCW contributes to soil (HMs) and water (toxic 
metals, N, P, COD) pollution. Similarly, the openly burned or incinerated 
HCWs generate HMs containing incineration ash. The external energy 
(electricity) and water are used in recycling of recyclable waste and 
steam sterilization for metallic reusable instruments, while bleaching 
powder in chemical disinfection. In addition, the diesel is used in 
incineration of HCWs. The incinerated ash is only dumped and there is 
no energy recovery system during incineration. In addition, there is no 
leachate and landfill gas collection system in the dumpsite and landfill.

2.2.4. Life cycle impact assessment
According to ISO 14040, the Life Cycle Impact Assessment (LCIA) tries 
to comprehend and assess the possible environmental impacts of HCWM 
systems, and their potential scale and relevance. The life cycle impacts at 
the midpoint and endpoint levels of this study were evaluated by 
SimaPro version 9.5.0.0 software. The ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint (H) 
V1.07/ World (2010) H and ReCiPe 2016 Endpoint (H) V1.07/ World 
(2010) H/A impact assessment methods were applied, respectively to 
calculate midpoint and endpoint environmental impacts connected to 
environmental emissions. The most common LCA software’s include

3.3. Heavy metals pollution from healthcare waste management
HCW is typically a complex mixture of different types of solid wastes. 
Most of the inorganic HCWs contain toxic HMs. Such HCW include 
thermometers, chemicals, plastics, PPE, syringes, needles, rubber, scal-
pels, metal cans, glass, battery and metallic tools. This research found 
several HMs in HCW such as Sb, As, Cd, Cr, Cu, Fe, Pb, Mn, Hg, Ni, Se, 
Ag, and Zn. Amidst, toxic HMs like As, Cd, Pb and Hg, are mostly present 
in plastic, syringes, rubber, adhesive plaster and battery. These HMs are 
transferred to the bottom ash when HCW are openly burned or incin-
erated. Incineration or open burning of HCW with high metallic content 
(e.g., Pb, Hg and Cd) can lead to the spread of toxic metals in the 
environment. Therefore, the treatment of HCW either by incinerating or 
open burning is a potential source of HMs pollution.
The concentrations of HMs are very high in HCW due to containing a 
major fraction of plastics and metallic wastes. If such waste is illegally 
dumped, then these HMs are deposited in the leachate and adjacent 
waterbodies. The leachate flow transfers deposited HMs to surrounding 
surface water and soil that subsequently reach to ground water and 
rivers. The recycling of HCW generates HMs through wastewater 
discharge. Thus, treatment and disposal of HCW shared to HMs pollution 
(see Fig. SI-4 (Supporting Information)). The results demonstrated that 
water is at risk of highly HMs pollution and then air and soil (see 
Table SI-10 (Supporting Information)). Finally, these HMs enter the food 
chain through the fish and crops/vegetables grown in the polluted water 
and soil. Here, comparatively high levels of the HMs release to the air, 
water and soil were from CHNH because it is a rural private clinic which 
disposed of the daily generated HCW illegally. Those who collect recy-
clable waste from CHNH recycle waste without any pretreatment. In 
addition, CMCH, USTC, MDC and PUHC demonstrated comparatively 
high levels of Fe & Hg, As & Mn, Cr, and Cd, Pb & Sn, respectively 
release to water from HCW disposal and treatment.
3.4. Discussion
LCA is not very old in developing countries. Lately, the researchers 
are trying to implement it in HCWM of developing countries, but the key 
problem in conducting LCA study is the absence of a database based on 
the actual scenarios of such countries (Alam and Mosharraf, 2019; Dey 
and Ray, 2020, 2021; Snigdha et al., 2023). This study compared 
existing HCWM systems between public and private HCFs through LCA. 
It demonstrated that private HCFs contributed higher than public ones 
in most of the studied impact categories except CMCH (prime HCF in 
Chattogram district). The inadequate HCWM system in private HCFs is 
highly responsible for surrounding water, soil and air pollution. The 
waste handlers are mainly affected by inadequate HCWM in low-income

of HCW recycling and treatment. Therefore, LCA based studies are 
supportive to identify the HCW disposal option with minimum impacts.
HCW is composed of 8 %–20 % plastics based on sources in 
Bangladesh. These SPWs are inevitably generated from HCFs (Ajaj et al., 
2023). The mismanagement of SPW resulted in MPs release that

Zhao and You, 2023). The crude dumping of SPWs along with me-

(Suzuki et al., 2022) and thus pollutes the aquatic environment. 
Therefore, SPWs release MPs during its handling, treatment and disposal 
(Guo et al., 2022). MPs can form volatile organic chemicals via photo- 
degradation and exert significant global warming, ecotoxicity, and air 
pollution effects. However, recycling of SPWs fast pyrolysis can effec-
tively minimize environmental damage by reducing ozone formation 
and air pollution by generating fuels and energy (Zhao and You, 2023). 
Previous studies found the higher MPs pollution impact from the recy-
cling of plastic HCW ranging from 6807 to 28,600 items/ kg plastic 
waste (Brown et al., 2023; Stapleton et al., 2023). However, the incin-
eration and dumping of HCW showed the comparatively lower MPs 
pollution. Silva et al. (2021) found up to 291 items/L of the HCW 
dumping leachate; however, the treatment of leachate can reduce MPs 
concentration. Similarly, Yang et al. (2021) observed 0.36–102 items/kg 
waste in bottom ash, while Shen et al. (2021) measured 23, 171, and 86 
items/kg in fly ash, bottom ash and soil.
HCW is one of the main sources of global HMs pollution. HMs are 
particularly released from HCWM during crude dumping, illegal 
disposal and landfill along with open burning and incineration ash. The 
dumped HCW make chemical reactions in the presence of sunlight and 
water, and then release the contained HMs. These HMs are distributed to 
surrounding water and soil through leachate flow. This study demon-
strated that HMs are released to air, water and soil from HCWM systems 
following the order as water > air > soil. Here, 5.89 µg Cd and 6.49 µg 
Co emission to air were found as the highest in City corporation clinics 
(CCCC) while other studied HMs were the highest in rural private clinic 
(CHNH). In soil emissions, only 0.81 µg Co was the highest from City 
corporation clinics (CCCC) while other studied HMs were the highest in 
private rural clinics (CHNH). Similarly, 14480 mg Fe and 0.71 mg Hg 
from CMCH, 0.8 mg As and 858.1 mg Mn from USTC, 0.03 mg Cr from 
MDC, 7.82 mg Cd and 259.6 mg Pb from PUHC, and other studied HMs 
(except Sb and Cr(VI)) from CHNH in water emissions. In addition, 7.58 
mg Sb and 4.32 mg Cr(VI) from the ideal HCWM system. Several studies 
demonstrated the high level of HMs content in HCW dumped soil and 
water as well released leachate (Dey and Ray, 2023; Janik-Karpinska 
et al., 2023). HMs from HCW are transferred to ash during the open 
burning or incineration. Therefore, previous studies identified excess 
levels of toxic HMs in burnt or incinerated ash. Regretfully, such HMs 
containing ash are disposed of in landfill without pretreatment in 
developing countries like Bangladesh (Adama et al., 2016; Dey and Ray, 
2021; Zhao et al., 2009). However, such HMs containing ash are 
disposed of in sanitary landfill after treatment or treated for reusing in 
construction or land development such as solidification/ stabilization in 
developed countries.

producing recycled products. Thus, generated MPs are ultimately

dumping can also release HMs to the environment. However, HMs in 
wastewater and leachate can be removed by chemical precipitation.
The potential primary MPs sources from plastics HCWM include 
mechanical recycling, incineration/open burning and illegal dumping. 
From recycling and incineration of plastic wastes, humans are exposed 
to MPs through air. However, humans are highly exposed to MPs 
through wastewater (recycling plant), incineration/burnt ashes, and 
leachate from dumping sites. Exposure to MPs in humans potentially 
leads to serious health issues, including various cancers, respiratory 
disorders, and inflammatory bowel disease. Understanding molecular 
mechanisms underlying the interaction of MPs with human cells may 
help extrapolate the risks to humans. There are several potential sources 
of HMs emissions from HCWM including dumping, open burning/ 
incineration and recycling. From these sources, HMs are released to 
water and soil. Furthermore, some HMs such as Pb and Hg are also 
released to air. From the environment HMs enter the human body 
through drinking water and taking foods grown in such contaminated 
soil/water, and directly inhaled from air. HMs can cause a variety of 
health problems in humans, including organ damage, cancer, and 
neurological disorders. Bioaccumulation of these HMs leads to a di-
versity of toxic effects on a variety of body tissues and organs. HMs 
disrupt cellular events including growth, proliferation, differentiation, 
damage-repairing processes, and apoptosis.
Considering the higher impacts from private HCFs, it is advisable for 
them to adopt strategies with minimum environmental footprints, HMs 
and MPs emissions from HCWM systems. In private HCFs, the adoption 
of appropriate source segregation of HCW, stream disinfection of in-
fectious and hazardous waste, controlled incineration and recycling of 
HCW can minimize environmental emissions from HCWM systems. The 
suggested strategies cannot be easily executed in HCFs, specifically in 
private HCFs. It will face several challenges such as sufficient funds, 
proper knowledge, skilled manpower, and advanced technologies. 
Therefore, it is required to provide free training to associated workers 
and staff of private HCFs by government or other concerned authorities. 
Government should give incentives to HCFs for adopting HCWM tech-
nologies in an eco-friendly way. The government formulated latest 
related regulations which also compel HCFs to adopt such strategies for 
safe HCWM. However, the poor implementation of such regulations 
discourages HCFs to adopt these strategies, and therefore these regula-
tions should be implemented strictly. These strategies can be effectively 
replicated in similar other HCFs and urban areas throughout the country 
and other developing countries.
Therefore, these findings show the potential environmental impacts, 
and level of MPs and HMs emissions which can facilitate in adopting the 
required controlling strategies. However, the available LCA software 
cannot measure MPs pollution due to the absence of a relevant database. 
Therefore, this study used the data from some relevant studies to 
calculate MPs emissions from HCWM.
4. Conclusion and future research direction
HCWM is a globally environmental concern. The developing coun-
tries like Bangladesh are at high risk due to possessing a major fraction 
of hazardous waste. Regretfully, these wastes are illegally disposed of 
with MSW in such countries that are liable for environmental pollution. 
Recently, LCA is considered as the best evaluation tool for environ-
mental burdens associated with HCWM. This study reveals that the 
current HCWM system differed based on sources and category of HCFs.

Private HCFs highly contributed to most of the studied impact categories 
(e.g., 1.12 kg CO2 eq. global warming, 1.01 kg 1,4-DCB terrestrial eco-
toxicity and 5.8 kg 1,4-DCB human non-carcinogenic toxicity) than 
public ones. It might be due to the uncontrolled and illegal disposal of all 
HCW by private HCFs, especially in rural areas where HCWM facilities 
are totally absent. Here, illegal recycling and open burning/ incineration 
are mainly responsible for human toxicity and global warming, while 
crude dumping and landfill are liable for ecotoxicity and eutrophication. 
The existing HCWM system can release HMs through illegal dumping, 
recycling plant wastewater and incineration ash. The water environ-
ment is more risk at HMs pollution than air and soil environments. 
Furthermore, this study demonstrated the higher quantity of MPs 
emission from the recycling of HCW that are accumulated in waste-
water. However, the factors responsible for MPs formation and their 
associated fate in the environment is still unexplored. Though it is 
possible to measure the impacts of SPWs through LCA but there is no 
specific way to measure MPs emission from different stages of HCWM. 
So, it is strongly suggested to develop LCA databases using actual sce-
narios of developing countries.
This study mainly used MPs data from literature. Plastic wastes are 
almost 100 % recycled in low-income countries like Bangladesh. Though 
HCW recycling is prohibited by WHO or other related international or-
ganizations. Currently, there is no LCA database for MPs emission from 
HCW disposal. Therefore, this study proposes some future research di-
rections to measure MPs emissions from plastic HCW disposal and 
subsequently, the emissions data will be used for LCA database formu-
lation, specifically for low-income communities. For MPs emission from 
plastic waste incineration, in a controlled way plastic waste will be 
incinerated to collect air samples and bottom ash. Then both samples 
will be analyzed in the laboratory for counting MPs contents in samples. 
Similarly, both air and wastewater samples will be collected from a 
recycling plant to measure MPs content which subsequently will be 
analyzed in the laboratory. In addition, mixed HCW including plastic 
waste will be dumped in sanitary landfill and dumping site, then 
leachate will be collected from both disposal points with time intervals 
7 days, 30 days, 90 days, 180 days and 365 days for MPs emissions 
counting both in dry and wet seasons. Finally, these experimental data of 
MPs from disposal of plastics HCW will be applied for related LCA 
database development. It is recommended that future studies should 
include a sensitivity analysis to assess the potential impact of excluding 
wastewater, gaseous emissions, and upstream processes. This analysis 
could provide insights into how these exclusions might affect the overall 
environmental impact assessment. For example, hypothetical scenarios 
could be developed to estimate the potential contributions of these 
excluded stages based on available literature or expert judgment. 
Similarly, future studies should consider a more comprehensive LCA 
that includes upstream processes. This would provide a more holistic 
view of the environmental impacts associated with HCWM, from prod-
uct manufacturing to final disposal.
