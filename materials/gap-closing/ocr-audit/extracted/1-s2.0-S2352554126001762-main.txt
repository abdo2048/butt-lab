ABSTRACT
Cigarette butts (CBs), the world's most abundant plastic litter, pose severe environmental threats 
due to their non-biodegradable cellulose acetate (CA) content. This study presents a circular 
economy solution by recovering CA from discarded CBs and co-electrospinning it with poly-
acrylonitrile (PAN) to fabricate high-performance air filtration membranes. Systematic optimi-
zation of PAN:CBs ratios and total solid content (8-18 wt%) revealed that hybrid nanofibers with 
10 wt% PAN and 6 wt% CBs achieved exceptional PM1.0 filtration efficiency (99.99%), low 
pressure drop (173 Pa), and a high quality factor (0.0558 Pa-1), outperforming pure PAN coun-
terparts. The incorporation of waste-derived CA not only enhanced specific surface area and fiber 
morphology but also reduced raw material costs by 27%. Coupled with a techno-economic 
assessment, this work demonstrates a scalable, eco-efficient pathway to transform hazardous 
urban waste into value-added functional materials, aligning with circular economy principles for 
simultaneous mitigation of plastic pollution and air quality degradation.
1. Introduction
Cigarette butts (CBs) represent the most discarded form of plastic litter globally, with an estimated 4.5 trillion discarded annually 
out of the 6 trillion cigarettes consumed worldwide (Torkashvand and Farzadkia, 2019). According to methodologically robust 
organizational audits, such as the Keep America Beautiful (2020) survey (Tobacco and the environment Apr. 26, 2023), estimates that 
this staggering volume translates to approximately 9.7 billion tons of waste, posing serious ecological and public health risks when 
improperly disposed of. Most CBs are made of cellulose acetate (CA), a type of non-biodegradable plastic that can persist in the 
environment for decades (Kurmus and Mohajerani, 2020). During this time, they leach harmful chemical substances, including 
nicotine, heavy metals (such as cadmium and lead), and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), into soil and aquatic ecosystems 
(Torkashvand and Farzadkia, 2019). As global cigarette consumption and cigarette waste continues to rise, effective management 
strategies remain challenging (De Fenzo et al., 2020). Therefore, CBs offer a compelling opportunity for circular economy initiatives,

The need for effective particulate matter (PM) filtration has grown increasingly urgent as air pollution levels rise, especially in 
urban areas (Flood-Garibay et al., 2023). PM, including PM10, PM2.5, and PM1.0, consist of microscopic airborne particles that pose 
serious health risks, such as lung cancer (Giammona et al., 2024), chronic respiratory conditions, and heart disease, leading to 
increased healthcare burdens and reduced life expectancy (Nakhjirgan et al., 2023). The World Health Organization (WHO) identifies 
PM as one of the most significant environmental health threats worldwide, with the heaviest burden falling on densely populated cities 
((WHO) 2021). To protect individuals from the harmful effects of PM, two approaches have been proposed: reducing PM emissions at 
the source and employing filtration methods. Comparatively, the latter method proves superior, offering a swift and effective means of 
shielding people from PM pollutants. In light of these severe public health consequences, there is an urgent need for innovative, 
high-performance, and sustainable air filtration technologies capable of efficiently capturing both fine and ultrafine particles. Such 
solutions are vital not only for safeguarding human health but also for addressing the wider environmental and economic costs 
associated with air pollution.
Aligned with the principle of transforming waste into environmental solutions, CBs have been repurposed across a wide range of 
application (Akid et al., 2025). Previous literature highlights their use in construction, where they serve as fillers or binders in bricks 
(Paulista et al., 2024), road paving (Guo et al., 2024), and insulation materials (Maderuelo-Sanz, 2021). In environmental engineering, 
CBs have been transformed into functional materials for removing pollutants from water, including organic contaminants (Premchand 
et al., 2025), oils (Doyan et al., 2021), dyes (Fahanwi et al., 2024), and heavy metals (Zhang et al., 2021). Much of these work focuses 
on converting CBs fibers into activated carbon via methods such as alkaline activation with microwave heating or low-temperature 
hydrothermal carbonization for applications like methylene blue adsorption (Dalmaz and S˙IVr˙IKaya ¨OZak, 2024). Despite these 
promising developments, the utilization of CBs-derived materials for air filtration remains notably underexplored. While electrospun 
nanofibers are well established for high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtration, most studies focus on biopolymers such as lignin, 
chitosan, poly (lactic acid) (Russo et al., 2022), polyvinvlyl alcohol (PVA) (Zhang et al., 2019), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) (Zhang 
et al., 2022) and polyacrylonitrile (PAN) (Lakshmanan et al., 2021). To date, only Kakoria et al. have demonstrated air filtration using 
cellulose acetate recovered from waste cigarette butts (Kakoria and Sinha-Ray, 2022). By employing supersonic solution blowing, they 
fabricated ultrafine nanofibers with a mean diameter of approximately 92.4 nm, achieving a filtration efficiency exceeding 99.7% 
against PM0.1. However, the rapid evaporation of acetone, a highly volatile and flammable solvent prone to forming explosive air 
mixtures, induced sporadic bead formation during processing. While the resulting membrane exhibited exceptional filtration per-
formance, these morphological defects may compromise fiber uniformity, mechanical robustness, and long-term operational stability, 
particularly during large scale manufacturing. In contrast, recent studies have successfully leveraged electrospinning to fabricate 
nanofibrous membranes from alternative feedstocks derived from waste. For instance, ¨Ozen et al., (2025) developed hemp hurd and 
polyvinyl alcohol composite membranes. Their optimal formulation with 15 wt% hemp hurd achieved filtration efficiencies of 98.74% 
for PM2.5 and 98.73% for PM10, alongside a quality factor of 0.049 inverse pascals. Similarly, Scaffaro et al., (2026) engineered a 
sandwich structured waste wool and polylactic acid membrane that delivered superior filtration efficiency with minimal pressure drop, 
yielding a higher quality factor of 0.079 inverse pascals. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that integrating materials derived 
from waste with biopolymers, particularly via electrospinning, can simultaneously enhance filtration efficiency, reduce pressure drop, 
and optimize the overall quality factor, offering a sustainable and scalable pathway for next generation air filters. Crucially, no study 
has yet explored the use of CBs-derived CA in conventional electrospun nanofiber membranes for air filtration, a critical gap, given that 
electrospinning offers superior control over fiber morphology, diameter, and alignment, enabling precise tuning of pore structure and 
surface functionality. The blending of CBs-derived CA with polyacrylonitrile (PAN) has the potential to create hybrid nanofiber 
membranes with enhanced air filtration properties. PAN, a widely used polymer in electrospinning, is prized for its excellent spinn-
ability, mechanical robustness, and thermal stability, together with good efficiency for gaseous pollutants filtration in respect to other 
polymer fibers (Zhang et al., 2023a). However, its reliance on petroleum-based sources and high production cost Kakorias remain 
limitations. Integrating waste-derived CA into PAN could reduce the demand for virgin polymer materials, while introducing polar 
ester and hydroxyl groups that may enhance surface-particle interactions, thereby improving filtration efficiency. Recent studies have 
suggested that the hybridization of natural fibers with synthetic polymers can lead to synergistic effects in nanofiber properties, 
including improved mechanical performance and filtration efficiency (Deng et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2016). Moreover, despite 
growing interest in waste-to-resource strategies, there remains a lack of systematic techno-economic assessments of CBs-derived air 
filtration materials. Critical performance indicators, such as filtration efficiency, pressure drop, quality factor, production cost, and 
dust-holding capacity, are essential for evaluating both functional efficacy and commercial viability. These metrics not only reflect a 
filter's ability to capture particulate matter under realistic operating conditions but also determine its service life, energy consumption, 
and scalability. To date, such comprehensive evaluations are largely absent in studies on CBs-based filters, highlighting an urgent need 
for integrated performance and cost analyses to bridge the gap between laboratory innovation and real-world deployment.
To address these gaps, this study presents the comprehensive investigation into the fabrication of hybrid PAN/CBs nanofiber 
membranes for air filtration applications. Given the potential toxicity of waste CBs, the cleaned samples were subjected to compre-
hensive characterization using FT-IR, GPC, XPS, ICP-MS, and GC-MS. These analyses confirmed the effective removal of contaminants 
and substantiated the environmental safety of the treated material. PAN serves as the structural matrix, and a series of PAN/CBs 
nanofiber membranes were fabricated via blend electrospinning under systematically varied formulation conditions: series A: fixed 
total concentrations of 8 wt% with varying PAN:CBs mass ratios 1:0 to 1:1, series B: fixed total concentrations of 10 wt%, and 14 wt% 
with varying PAN:CBs mass ratios 1:0 and 1:1, series C: PAN-only solutions at concentrations 8-18 wt% in 2 wt% increments, no CBs, 
and series D: fixed PAN mass 8, 10, 14 wt% with increasing CBs to push total solids to 18 wt%. Within the described series of

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Chemical reagents
Cigarette butts were collected from cigarette butts’ collectors located in restaurants, walking road, and buildings from the campus 
of Shandong University of Science and Technology. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Mw = 150,000 g/mol), Cellulose acetate (CA, 
Mw = 30,000 g/mol) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China, whereas N, N- 
dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (China), anhydrous ethanol (EtOH, 
99.7% w/w) was purchased from Tianjin Fuyu Fine Chemical Co., Ltd, Tianjin, China., and deionized water was self-made by the 
laboratory. All the materials and reagents were used as received without any purification.

2.2. Extraction and characterization of CA from waste CBs
The procedure depicted in Fig. 1 outlines the method for extracting CA from discarded CBs. The discarded CBs were manually 
stripped to retrieve the cotton cores. These cores were then carefully cut into coarse fiber segments measuring 1-2 cm, which contained 
contaminants including nicotine, heavy metals, and priority polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), thereby requiring further 
treatment. To obtain pristine regenerated CA, the extracted material underwent a series of pressing, soaking, and washing cycles using 
deionized water (at a ratio of 1 g CBs to 10 mL water) followed by anhydrous ethanol (at a ratio of 1 g CBs to 20 mL anhydrous ethanol) 
for a designated period, repeated at least 5-6 times until all traces of dirt were eradicated to solubilize and extract hydrophobic 
contaminants such as nicotine. Ethanol was chosen for its selectivity toward organic impurities while preserving CA polymer integrity. 
Once the remnants were thoroughly cleansed, a final rinse with distilled water was administered to eliminate any residual ethanol and 
water-soluble toxins that may prevent re-adsorption of contaminants during drying. The soaked fibers, or remnants, were then sub-
jected to a drying process in a vacuum dryer set at 65◦C for approximately 7-8 h, eliminating any remaining moisture and evaporating 
the lingering solvent, thereby yielding pure regenerated cellulose acetate segments. Higher temperatures might induce deacetylation 
or polymer chain scission. It is of utmost importance to consistently cover the recycled remnants when not in use to prevent evapo-
ration of the cellulose acetate. All work should be conducted under a fume hood, and basic protective gear should be worn, including 
gloves, a mask, and safety goggles, as even small amounts of residual nicotine can be harmful.
Once purified, recycled CA must pass safety checks to ensure no harmful residues remain. To verify this requirement, X-ray 
photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher ESCALAB Xi+) was used to analyze the elemental composition and chemical structure of 
the CBs. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (Agilent 7800 ICP-MS, USA) was performed to determine the concentrations of 
heavy metals, specifically arsenic (As), chromium (Cr), lead (Pb), and cadmium (Cd), in the CBs’ samples. Gas Chromatography-Mass 
Spectrometry (ISQ7000+TRACE1300) was employed to quantify the concentrations of 16 PAHs in CBs before and after the cleaning 
process. The molecular weight distribution of CA obtained from discarded CBs was measured via Gel Permeation Chromatography 
(GPC) on a PL-GPC120 (Agilent Technologies, USA) instrument equipped with a refractive index (RI) detector.
2.3. Membrane fabrication
PAN and CBs polymer solutions were prepared under four different series (series A: fixed total concentrations of 8 wt% with varying 
PAN:CBs mass ratios 1:0 to 1:1, series B: fixed total concentrations 10, and 14 wt% with varying PAN:CBs mass ratios 1:0 and 1:1, series 
C: PAN concentrations 8-18 wt% in 2 wt% increments, no CBs and series D: fixed PAN mass 8, 10, 14 wt% with increasing CBs to push 
total solids to 18 wt%) by dissolving in DMF at 68◦C under constant stirring for at least 7 h. During this time, ensure that the mixture 
thoroughly blends and dissolves, to form a homogeneous mixture. Allow the liquid to rest for a brief period before extracting it using a 
syringe for the spinning process. Table S1 summarizes specific electrospinning parameters for PAN/CBs electrospinning, whereas the 
whole procedure is shown in Fig. 1. A 3 mL spinning solution was electrospun directly onto non-woven paper. The PAN/CBs nanofiber 
was placed in vacuum dryer for at least 7 h at 60◦C to completely evaporate the solvent. In addition, a selected nanofiber membrane 
(prepared from the PAN/CBs 8 wt% solution with a 1:1 PAN:CBs mass ratio) was subjected to heat-press post-treatment using a paper 
laminator (M&G stationary Inc. China) at a temperature of 100◦C, 120◦C, and 140◦C, to investigate its effect on fiber morphology and 
filtration performance.
2.4. Membrane characterization
Surface morphology and elemental distribution of the nanofiber membranes were characterized using field-emission scanning 
electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Regulus 8220, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, ULTIM-Max, 
Oxford Instruments, UK). Quantitative analysis of fiber diameter was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, 
USA) by randomly measuring at least 50 individual fibers per image across three representative fields of view, and data are reported as 
mean ± standard deviation (SD). Pore size distribution was determined by applying consistent thresholding parameters to delineate 
pore boundaries in SEM images, followed by conversion of pore area to equivalent circular diameter, with results expressed as mean 
pore diameter ± SD based on analysis of no fewer than 100 pores per sample. Membrane surface wettability was indicated by static 
water contact angle (WCA) measured on a goniometer (Shanghai, Zhongchen, JC2000D2). The samples were dried sufficiently before 
the measurement, then a dangling droplet of 1 μL of deionized water was carefully dropped on the sample's surface for at least five 
times at different position. Three positions on a single sample were randomly chosen for measuring and the mean value was used as the 
test result. Membrane surface zeta potential was measured on an electrokinetic (SurPASS 3, Anton Paar) with 1 mM KCl solution as 
background solution, 0.05 mol/L HCl and 0.05 mol/L NaOH were used to adjust the pH from 9.5 to 3. Each sample was tested three 
times and the average was taken as the measured result. The chemical compositions of samples were characterized by a Fourier 
transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS10, Thermo Scientific, USA) between 400 and 4000 cm-1 to confirm the chemical 
integrity of PAN and the successful incorporation of cigarette butt-derived components (CA). The mechanical properties were per-
formed on a Floor Model Universal Testing System (5300, Instron Corporation, USA). During the test, samples of 130 × 25 mm size 
were clamped at both ends with 100 mm of interval and pulled in tension, the tensile speed was 100 mm/min, at least five specimens 
were tested for each sample. Surface areas were calculated using the Branauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) nitrogen absorption-desorption 
method. Membrane thickness of each sample were measured using a digital micrometer at multiple points (n ≥ 10) across the sam-
ple to ensure consistency and the average was taken as the measure result. Finally, the porosities (ε) of the electrospun PAN and PAN/ 
CB nanofiber membranes were determined using a gravimetric liquid-displacement method with anhydrous ethanol. Specifically, Wwet

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Sustainable evaluation of recycled CA from waste CBs
Two independent extraction trials were conducted to recover CA from waste CBs, and the corresponding yields are presented in 
Fig. S1. Both trials achieved a consistent recovery rate of approximately 79.9% CA, demonstrating high reproducibility under the 
applied experimental conditions. This consistent yield indicates that the selected solvent system and extraction protocol effectively and 
selectively dissolve CA while leaving behind non-acetate components such as paper, residual tobacco, and filter additives. The 
reproducibility across both trials further underscores the robustness of the method, supporting its potential for scale-up in recycling 
initiatives aimed at valorizing CBs waste.
FT-IR spectra of the recovered CA from both batches were compared with those of pure CA, as shown in Fig. 3. Key characteristic 
bands of cellulose acetate are clearly evident in the recycled samples. Notably, the strong and sharp carbonyl (C––O) stretching vi-
bration of the acetate group appears at 1734 cm-1, an isolated and diagnostic region in the FT-IR spectrum (Yue et al., 2022). Additional 
characteristic peaks include the asymmetric C–O–C stretching (associated with ester linkages in the glycosidic backbone) at 1215 cm-1, 
the C–O stretching vibration of the ester linkage at 1032 cm-1, and C–H bending vibrations at 1367 cm-1. A weak O–H stretching band is 
observed around 3500 cm-1, consistent with partial retention of hydroxyl groups; however, its reduced intensity reflects the high 
degree of acetylation, confirming successful substitution of hydroxyl groups during CA synthesis. Therefore, the spectral similarity 
between the recycled material and pure CA confirms that the extracted product retains the essential chemical structure of virgin CA, 
supporting its potential as a viable substitute in future applications (Torkashvand et al., 2021). Furthermore, GPC analysis of CA 
extracted from waste CBs, as shown in Table S2, was conducted using DMF as the mobile phase and calibrated against narrow 
polystyrene (PS) standards. The average molecular weights, reported as polystyrene-equivalent values, are: number-average molecular 
weight (Mn) = 52,515 g/mol, weight-average molecular weight (Mw) = 180,382 g/mol, and peak molecular weight (Mp) = 150, 
908 g/mol. The polydispersity index (PDI = Mw/Mn) is 3.43, indicating a broad molecular weight distribution, commonly observed in 
polymers recovered from post-consumer waste due to partial degradation during use, environmental exposure, or extraction process 
variability. These molecular weights are sufficient for electrospinning, though the high dispersity may affect solution viscosity and 
fiber uniformity.
To comprehensively verify safety compliance of the recycled cellulose acetate, surface chemistry, heavy metal content, and organic 
contaminants were systematically characterized by XPS, ICP-MS, and GC-MS, respectively. XPS analysis was firstly employed to 
determine surface chemical composition of CBs before and after cleaning. The C 1s spectra of used and cleaned CBs reveal dramatic 
differences (Figures S2 (a-1) and (b-1)). For used CBs, the deconvoluted C 1s spectrum exhibits two peaks: 284.66 eV assigned to C–C/ 
C–H bonds from adsorbed polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and tar deposits, and 285.54 eV attributed to C–N bonds from 
nicotine and nitrogenous tobacco combustion products. Notably, characteristic cellulose acetate peaks are completely obscured by 
smoke-derived contaminants, indicating extensive surface coverage by hydrophobic tar residues. In contrast, the C 1s spectrum of 
cleaned CBs exhibits three well-resolved deconvoluted peaks corresponding to cellulose acetate: 284.65 eV (C–C/C–H bonds in the CA 
polymer backbone), 286.49 eV (C–O functional groups, including ether linkages and secondary hydroxyls), and 288.87 eV (ester 
carbonyl of acetate groups). The N 1s spectra provide direct evidence of nitrogenous contaminant removal (Figure S2 (a-2) and (b-2). 
Used CBs exhibit a strong N 1s signal comprising two components: 399.25 eV (pyridinic/pyrrolic nitrogen from nicotine) and 
400.33 eV (amide nitrogen from protein-derived compounds). Following cleaning, the N 1s signal intensity decreases by 90-95%, with 
only a weak residual peak at 398.72 eV (pyridinic/pyrrolic N). This near-complete elimination demonstrates the cleaning protocol's 
high efficacy in extracting nicotine and other nitrogen-containing contaminants, confirming the material's suitability for nanofiber 
membrane fabrication with surface chemistry comparable to virgin cellulose acetate. This finding is further corroborated by the GC-MS 
quantification of nicotine and 16 priority PAHs in CBs before and after cleaning (Table S3). The results demonstrate the highly effective 
removal of nicotine and low-to-medium molecular weight PAHs, with removal efficiencies exceeding 97% for most detectable com-
pounds, many of which fell below the limit of detection (LOD = 0.20 μg/g) post-cleaning. Notably, although the removal efficiencies

3.2.1.2. Membrane characterization and mechanism analysis. Following the evaluation of filtration performance in Fig. 4, a compre-
hensive suite of physicochemical and structural characterizations was carried out to elucidate the underlying mechanisms governing 
the observed trends in particle capture efficiency, pressure drop, and quality factor. SEM was employed to investigate how the 
incorporation of CBs-derived components influences the morphology, diameter, and surface architecture of PAN-based nanofibers at a 
fixed total concentration of 8 wt%, while varying the PAN-to-CBs weight ratio. As shown in Fig. 5(a–g), the electrospun fibers maintain 
continuous and smooth morphologies across all compositions, from pure PAN (1:0) to a 1:1 PAN/CBs blend, indicating good dispersion 
of CBs components within the PAN/DMF solution and compatibility during electrospinning. However, a clear reduction in average 
fiber diameter is observed, decreasing from 120.91 nm (1:0) to 72.93 nm (1:1), as showed in Fig. S3. This thinning effect is consistent 
with established literature indicating that carbon-based additives incorporation can reduce polymer solution viscosity and enhance 
charge density on the electrospinning jet, thereby intensifying stretching forces in the electric field and yielding finer fibers (Jo et al., 
2024). A similar trend is seen in pore size (Fig. S4), which contracts from 0.62 μm (1:0) to 0.45 μm (1:1), further confirming the 
densification of the fibrous network with higher CBs content. Post-filtration SEM images, as illustrated in Fig. 5(h–i), reveal that the 
PAN/CBs (1:1) membrane appears more heavily loaded with captured particles compared to pure PAN (1:0), with visible fiber 
bending, breakage, and displacement, suggesting greater particle adhesion, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Paradoxically, despite this apparent 
“dirtier” appearance, its PM1.0 filtration efficiency is slightly lower than that of pure PAN. This counterintuitive result underscores a 
critical principle in filtration science: performance is not solely determined by particle retention but also by airflow dynamics and 
structural integrity. The denser, thinner-fiber structure of the 1:1 membrane leads to faster clogging and significantly higher resistance 
to airflow, as corroborated by the elevated pressure drop in Fig. 4(c), making it less energy-efficient despite its high initial capture 
capacity (Zhang et al., 2019). EDS mapping illustrated in Fig. 5(j–k) provides direct evidence of molecular-level integration between 
PAN and CBs components. Nitrogen (N), characteristic of PAN's nitrile groups, and oxygen (O), primarily from cellulose acetate in CBs, 
are co-localized across individual fibers. With increasing CBs content (from 0% to 50%), N intensity decreases while O increases, 
confirming uniform blending rather than phase separation (Lakshmanan et al., 2021).

(PAN/CBs (1:1)), with the exception of the 1:0.8 sample (32 μm), as shown in Fig. 7(a). At this intermediate loading, the addition of 
cellulose acetate from CBs may transiently reduce solution viscosity or alter jet stretching dynamics, yielding finer fibers and/or lower 
deposition rates. However, this trend was reproducible across ≥5 independent electrospinning batches (Table S5). And, even with 
substantial incorporation of hydrophilic cellulose acetate, the membranes retain strong hydrophobicity, as evidenced by water contact

and sustainability across varying operational conditions.
At the lowest airflow velocity of 3.3 cm/s, the PAN/CBs membranes demonstrate significant techno-economic advantages over the 
pure PAN reference. Notably, blends with higher CBs content exhibit substantially enhanced dust-holding capacities. For instance, the 
PAN 10 wt% + CBs 6 wt% formulation (Fig. 18(d)) achieves a maximum dust capacity ratio of 2.07, while PAN 8 wt% + CBs 10 wt%

for low-velocity applications, such as indoor air purification systems and residential HVAC filters.

remains the most promising, maintaining a high dust capacity ratio (1.47) and a favorable QF ratio (0.65), albeit reduced from lower 
velocities. In contrast, PAN 8 wt% + CBs 8 wt% (Fig. 18(b)) shows a more modest improvement in dust capacity (0.83×) and a lower 
QF ratio (0.34), while PAN 8 wt% + CBs 10 wt% underperforms with only half the dust capacity of the reference. Nevertheless, all 
tested blends continue to offer 18–27% cost savings, and filtration efficiency remains virtually unchanged (η ratios ≈ 0.999-1.001). 
Pressure drop ratios increase moderately (1.08-1.69), suggesting that although these membranes remain functional at moderate flow 
rates, their resistance to clogging and overall filtration quality decline relative to low-velocity operation. This indicates a sensitivity to 
airflow dynamics, where structural integrity and pore stability become critical factors.

The sustained performance over time and across varying flow rates indicates that the nanofiber membrane is highly resistant to

3.5. A sustainable circular approach: transforming discarded CBs into high-performance nanofiber membranes for air and water filtration
The development of hybrid polyacrylonitrile/cellulose acetate from cigarette butts (PAN/CBs) nanofiber membranes represents a 
sustainable and circular strategy to address the global challenge of cigarette butt waste, one of the most prevalent forms of litter 
worldwide. As illustrated in Fig. 20, this study presents a closed-loop process that converts discarded CBs, commonly found in public 
spaces, beaches, and landfills, into high-performance filtration materials, thereby transforming environmental pollutants into valuable 
resources. The process begins with the collection of used CBs from urban and natural environments, preventing their accumulation in 
ecosystems and reducing pollution. Collected CBs undergo chemical pretreatment to recover CA, the primary component of cigarette 
filters. This involves sorting, washing with absolute ethanol to remove tar, nicotine, and other toxic residues, followed by rinsing and 
drying. Critically, the ethanol used in this step is recovered via distillation and reused, minimizing solvent waste and supporting green 
processing principles. The recovered CA is then blended with PAN as a renewable, low-cost additive and electrospun into nanofiber 
membranes. This approach not only reduces reliance on virgin polymers but also enables the upcycling of waste into functional 
materials through scalable and sustainable fabrication. The resulting PAN/CBs nanofiber membranes exhibit excellent structural and 
surface properties suitable for dual applications in air and liquid filtration. In air filtration, the as-spun membranes demonstrate strong 
hydrophobicity (water contact angle >120◦), high porosity, and a fine fibrous network, enabling efficient capture of airborne par-
ticulate matter, including PM1.0, PM2.5, and PM10, with minimal pressure drop. These characteristics make them ideal candidates for 
use in indoor air purifiers, HVAC systems, and personal protective face masks, offering effective protection against airborne pollutants. 
For liquid filtration, zeta potential measurements (Figure S21 and Table S6) reveal that the membranes possess a predominantly 
negative surface charge across a wide pH range (pH 5-9), particularly under neutral and alkaline conditions. This intrinsic negative 
charge enhances electrostatic repulsion against negatively charged contaminants such as bacteria, organic molecules, and colloidal 
particles commonly present in wastewater and natural water sources. Consequently, the membranes exhibit improved fouling resis-
tance and stable filtration performance, making them highly suitable for microfiltration and ultrafiltration applications. Furthermore, 
the retained negative charge facilitates compatibility with thin-film composite (TFC) membrane fabrication (Gohain et al., 2024; 
Luthfiana et al., 2025; Mahto et al., 2022). A simple NaOH post-treatment renders the support layer hydrophilic, enabling uniform 
deposition of a polyamide selective layer, a critical step in producing high-flux, durable TFC membranes. In membrane distillation 
(MD) for desalination, the hydrophobic nature of the PAN/CBs membranes (WCA >120◦) ensures efficient separation of clean water 
vapor from saline or contaminated feed solutions. Their robust water-repellent surface prevents wetting, even under harsh operating 
conditions, thereby enhancing long-term stability and performance in saline environments. Finally, at end-of-life, the membranes can 
be collected and recycled either through chemical reclamation or repurposing, completing the circular lifecycle. This closed-loop 
system, from waste collection to membrane fabrication, application, and recycling, demonstrates a full circular economy model: 
waste CBs → green processing → high-value membranes → dual air and water filtration → reuse/recycling. By converting a ubiquitous 
pollutant into multifunctional filtration materials, this work advances the integration of waste management and environmental 
technology. It offers a practical, scalable, and environmentally responsible solution that simultaneously supports cleaner air, sus-
tainable water treatment, and reduced plastic pollution. The proposed strategy exemplifies how circular innovation can drive both 
environmental stewardship and technological advancement, aligning closely with the goals of resource conservation and sustainable 
development.
4. Concluding remarks and future prospect
This study demonstrates that discarded cigarette butts can be upcycled into high-performance hybrid nanofiber membranes for air 
filtration, effectively transforming a ubiquitous plastic waste stream into a valuable raw material. The integration of cigarette butt- 
derived cellulose acetate with polyacrylonitrile via blend electrospinning establishes a scalable pathway that simultaneously en-
hances filtration performance and reduces raw material costs. The optimal formulation (10 wt% PAN and 6 wt% CBs) achieves near- 
complete removal of fine particulate matter while maintaining a low pressure drop, validating both the technical and economic 
feasibility of integrating waste-derived polymers. Importantly, this performance enhancement is accompanied by a 27% reduction in 
raw material costs compared to virgin PAN, highlighting the strong economic incentives for utilizing waste-derived polymers. 
Furthermore, integrating comprehensive filtration testing with a techno-economic analysis of CB-based air filters provides critical 
evidence supporting their industrial scalability. Therefore, the present findings provide a compelling foundation for scaling CB 
valorization as a practical and sustainable solution within modern materials management frameworks.
However, the practical implementation of this approach requires overcoming several real-world challenges. First, the nanofiber 
membrane filters must be thoroughly evaluated to ensure they maintain high performance over extended periods under fluctuating 
humidity, temperature, and continuous exposure to complex air pollutants. Second, establishing consistent quality control for CB 
collection, developing scalable production methods, and ensuring the safe disposal or recycling of spent filters are essential to prevent 
secondary microplastic pollution and comply with environmental regulations. Finally, although heat-pressing enhances the me-
chanical robustness of the membranes, this post-treatment step requires further optimization for large-scale manufacturing to ensure 
structural stability under continuous airflow. Therefore, future research should prioritize full-scale life cycle assessments to evaluate 
the true environmental and economic impacts of these nanofiber membrane filters from production to end-of-life. Pilot-scale 
manufacturing trials are necessary to increase production throughput, ensure consistent fiber quality, and optimize post-treatment 
parameters under continuous operation. Additionally, exploring surface functionalization or catalytic doping could further expand 
the utility of these membranes to include the capture of harmful volatile organic compounds or antimicrobial filtration. Concurrently, 
collaboration with policymakers is essential to establish performance standards, support reliable recycling supply chains, and integrate
