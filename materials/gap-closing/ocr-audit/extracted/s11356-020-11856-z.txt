REVIEW ARTICLE
Toxicity of cigarette butts and possible recycling solutions —a
literature review
Ivan Moroz1 & Luiz G. B. Scapolio 2 & Ivana Cesarino1 & Alcides L. Leão 1 & Giuliano Bonanomi3
Received: 3 August 2020 / Accepted: 26 November 2020
# The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH, DE part of Springer Nature 2021
Abstract
Tobacco is a worldwide-consumed product, which in addition to causing public health-related issues is responsible for the most
common form of litter in the world—smoked cigarette butts (CBs). A large attention has been drawn to this question, since this
specific waste type tends to end up in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems, posing serious threat to a range of life forms.
Decomposition may take several years to occur because cellulose acetate is hardly accessible, before deacetylation, by bacteria
and fungi. This review concerns the toxicity derived from smoked cigarette butts, as well as innovative ecological solutions for
solving the CB litter problem. Toxicity studies have demonstrated the critical influence of chemicals present in smoked CBs to
the environment as a whole, but also the physical contaminating potential considering micro- and nanoparticles derived from CB
material. Nevertheless, several technological approaches were aimed at unveiling hidden value within used CBs, as well as
propositions for incorporation of this residue in large volume production items or direct recycling. In summary, several methods
are available to alleviate CB pollution, while appropriate and efficient collection logistics by consumers appears as the main
bottleneck for an effective recycling. It is also clear that while considerable progress has been made recently in light of CB
recycling solutions, there is still a vast research capacity in this regard.
Keywords Cigarette butt . Toxicity . Decomposition . Recycling waste . Pollution . Debris
Introduction
Direct disposing of non-degradable residues into the environ-
ment is a major pollution issue worldwide. During their exis-
tence, these residues have the potential to harm nature in a
myriad of ways. Among them, cigarette butts (CBs) are an
important part of urban litter because of their massive numbers
and of their longevity. According to the 2019 World Health
Organization Report on the Global Tobacco Epidemic (WHO
Report on the Global Tobacco Epidemic 2019), an estimated
1.1 billion people of 15 years of age and over are considered
tobacco users, of which 1.07 billion are considered smokers.
Interestingly, this number has declined since the first estimate
in 2007, when 1.46 billion people were considered tobacco
users. It is easy to imagine the massive amounts of cigarette
butts produced over time. Indeed, according to the 2018
Tobacco Atlas (Drope et al. 2018), in 2016 only, about 5.7
trillion cigarettes were smoked worldwide. Public health is-
sues associated with tobacco use and smoking habits have
been extensively researched and discussed, and will be not
addressed in this paper, but it is worth noting that over 7.1
million deaths were associated with tobacco use in 2016 alone
(Drope et al. 2018).
A large proportion of these cigarettes, once smoked, is
simply thrown into the ground in the form of a CB, which
can still retain unburnt or partially burnt tobacco; eventually,
this litter may end up in marine environments as well
(Loizidou et al.2018). Even if a CB has no remaining tobacco,
toxic chemicals derived from the smoking process remain
trapped in the butt. Moreover, CBs pose serious environmen-
tal threats because of their resistance to biodegradation in

plasticized cellulose acetate, compressed and externally
wrapped with a paper layer (Fig. 1a, b ). In fact, cellulose
acetate is a material that needs a deacetylation step to initiate
biodegradation process (Puls et al. 2011), and therefore re-
quires the addition of other components to enhance natural

Bonanomi et al. (2015, 2020) performed extensive research
on this matter, using artificially smoked CBs and different
environment conditions (laboratory and open field) to evalu-
ate mass loss and CB carbon quality alteration for certain time
intervals. They reported that CB decomposition involves three
stages: an initial fast decay of 15% to 20% mass loss in the
first 30 days, attributed to the degradation of external involv-
ing paper; then a slow 2-year decomposition period which
accounts for approximately 10% mass loss and is rather inde-
pendent of surrounding conditions; and finally the last stage
where environmental conditions affect decomposition rate
significantly (Bonanomi et al. 2020). Considering all condi-
tions tested, CB mass loss after 5 years ranged from 50 to
80%. These results prove that CBs are both short- and long-
term toxicity threats because leaching occurs rapidly when
CBs are wetted but also occurs over a long period with peri-
odic wettings and slow decomposition.
As an alternative for common cellulose acetate filters, com-
panies have produced filters made only of cellulose, which in
theory should degrade much faster. However, according to a
study published by Joly and Coulis (2018), the environment in
which decomposition takes place influences its rate and Green
et al. ( 2020) reported toxic effects in invertebrates caused by
biodegradable CBs. Thus, the substitution for these biode-
gradable filters should be accompanied by user knowledge
and responsibility concernin g disposal habits. Robertson
et al. ( 2012) approached this issue from a different perspec-
tive. Since the hydrolysis of cellulose acetate into cellulose
and acetic acid is a process which limits the decomposition
rate, these authors proposed the use of encapsulated,
controlled-release acid catalysts to accelerate the process, once
they are exposed to environmental water.

2014 by Roder Green et al. ( 2014). The authors observed the
characteristics of butt littering in the city of Berlin. Because of

by shop owners, for example), and accumulation in spots
not attended by mechanical cleaning, it was found that
littering is heterogeneous: average CB concentration was 2.7
butts m−2, while the maximum concentration found was 48.8
butts m−2. The elution kinetics of nicotine in water was also a
target of attention. The authors proposed two situations: one,
simulating a puddle of standing water, containing cigarette
butts, and the other, simulating a rainfall experience over
CBs, in which there is the influence of water movement. In
the first situation, one CB was added to 1 L of purified water.
After several different time intervals, aliquots were taken and
nicotine content was measured. Twenty-four-hour exposure
resulted in a total release of 7.1 mg g −1 of nicotine, a value
14 times higher than the 0.5 mg g −1 threshold established by
the European Union for hazardous tobacco waste. Also, the
dynamics of elution showed that 50% of all the nicotine was
released in 27.2 min, and 90% after 10 h. This fact is alarming
because it stresses how fast this contamination occurs. The
rainfall experiment consisted of placing five CBs on a 0.16-
m2 stainless steel tray, positioned with a 2% slope (equivalent
to common sidewalk slopes). A total of 15 daily simulated
rainfall events were conducted to meet an estimate of the
minimum quantity of water that comes in contact with littered
CBs due to rainfall. Results showed a total release of 3.8 mg
g−1 of nicotine, after 15 events that totalled a precipitation of
21.6 mm; 50% of nicotine release occurred after 1.6 mm of
precipitation, equivalent to only 1.1 rainfall events. Finally,
after the 15 rainfall events, the remaining CBs were subjected
to the conditions of the standing water experiment.
Surprisingly, even after leaching nicotine during the rainfall
experiments, these CBs still leached 0.16 mg g −1 of nicotine
after 24-h exposure to standing water, still above threshold
limits for the EU, showing that even CBs which were
“washed” by rainfall are still an ecological threat to aquatic
environments.
The creation of value for CBs plays a role in reducing
reckless disposal of this item, and therefore contributes to
prevent environmental degradation. According to
Torkashvand and Farzadkia (2019), three strategies are mainly
used for controlling CB littering: establishment of fines and
waste tax; creating responsibility for consumers and pro-
ducers; and raising awareness about the disadvantages of
CBs. If these residues were to be considered a source material
with possible financial return, an additional driving force for
CB collection would be created almost as a “fourth strategy”
of CB littering control.
One can conclude that the permanence of CBs packed with
toxic chemicals in the environment is a serious issue, with
urgency to its resolution. In addition to the secondary
chemicals present in cigarettes and cellulose acetate litters,
also nicotine and many other chemical compounds can cause
toxic effects on diverse organisms as well. Here, we review
available studies that investigate the toxic effect of CBs on a
range of organisms and the possible solutions for effective
recycling of this waste.
Materials and methods
Literature search and data collection
The search protocol involved articles published up to October
2020, concerning the toxic effect of CBs and potential
recycling solutions. The search was carried out in all interna-
tional journals using online versions of the Web of Science,
Science Direct, and Scopus. Keywords used were “cigarette
butt”, “cigarette litter”,a n d“ cigarette filter”, and the search
was confined to the article title taking care to include also
plural variations (butts and filters). Criteria for article selection
were decided a priori to avoid personal bias; only articles
containing quantitative data on CBs toxicity were included.
Basically, the screening process was comprised of the follow-
ing stages:
(1) Title and abstract: a simple evaluation of both title and
abstract was carried out for every result, in relation to
cigarette butt toxicity and/or possible applications for
cigarette butts to exclude non-related results;
(2) Abstract: articles filtered in (1) had their abstract fully
read, in order to further avail the real interest for this
review article and filter out non-applicable studies;
(3) Full content: results filtered from (2) had their full con-
tent read and evaluated;
(4) Extensive evaluation of every document present in
“References” of articles filtered from (2).
Results and discussion
A large number of results was returned for the defined title
search strings: “cigarette butt” resulted in approximately 300
articles, “cigarette litter” was related to 12 papers and “ciga-
rette filter” totalled over 400 results. After completion of the
screening process, research was focused down to 87 refer-
ences, 29 of which related to cigarette butt toxicity and 58
associated with potential recycling applications of this waste.

The potential for heavy metal leaching from discarded CBs
was addressed elsewhere (Moriwaki et al. 2009; Moerman
and Potts 2011; Dobaradaran et al. 2017; Dobaradaran et al.
2018), and CBs have been demonstrated to be also a source of
polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) contamination to the
environment (Dobaradaran et al. 2019; Dobaradaran et al.
2020). The changes in metal concentrations due to exposure
to CBs also affect the diversity and distribution of microbial
populations present in coastal sediments, as reported by
Quéméneur et al. ( 2020). Even if CBs are the focus of this
article, it should be noted that recent research by Mansouri
et al. ( 2020) determined that cigarette ash can be more toxic
than CBs indicating the importance of controlling this waste
as well.
Araújo and Costa ( 2019) described detrimental aspects of
CB littering and accumulation in coastal areas in their recent
review article, as well as information regarding the effective-
ness of campaigns and actions towards CB pollution aware-
ness and reduction. Another article (Loizidou et al. 2018)
attempted to answer the question “Are regular beach clean-
ups by local authorities efficient? ”, and showed that for nine
beaches located in Cyprus, even after authorities cleaning pro-
cedures, small pieces of litter (including CBs) still remained
on the beach.
The productivity of farm crops could be hindered by CB
toxicity, as described by Green et al. ( 2019). These authors
reported that littered CBs caused reduction in shoot length and
germination success of both perennial ryegrass and white clo-
ver, with potential harm to short-term productivity of these
species. Food contamination is also possible. In fact, Selmar
et al. ( 2018) reported that inappropriate disposal of CBs in
agricultural fields by farm workers cause a substantial contam-
ination of vegetable production due to nicotine uptake by
plants. The new pathway of pollution was recorded with a
single CB per square metre, with detectable alkaloid leaching
into the soil and subsequent uptake by different crops. In this
paper, we focus on adverse effects on life forms, including
potential indirect damage to human species.
The ubiquity of CB litter is responsible for turning CB into
a new waste stream. As informed by Rebischung et al. (2018),
the European List of Wastes does not have a specific entry for
cigarette butts; instead, it is attached to an entry defined as
“Municipal wastes including separately collected fraction/
separately collected fraction/other fractions not otherwise
specified”. The growing conscience about CB toxicity high-
lights the need for determining which of the fifteen hazardous
properties (HP)—a series of hazard classes according to the
effect caused, defined in the Waste Framework Directive (EU
2008)—would apply in order to complete its classification as a
separate kind of waste. The authors centred their study on the
most relevant hazard characteristics for this residue: HP4 (ir-
ritant—skin irritation and eye damage), HP5 (specific target
organ toxicity/aspiration toxicity), HP6 (acute toxicity), HP7
(carcinogenic), HP8 (corrosive), HP10 (toxic for reproduc-
tion), HP11 (mutagenic), HP1 3 (sensitising), and HP14
(ecotoxic). Tests were conducted according to applicable reg-
ulatory documents for three samples, obtained from CB-
collecting companies in France. Two samples were dry, and
one was wet. Chemical analyses were also performed to eval-
uate all known pollutants of CB. Results indicated that sam-
ples would be classified as hazardous only according to hazard
characteristics HP6 (acute toxicity) and HP14 (ecotoxic).
Concerning HP6, the authors state that nicotine is the main
chemical responsible for toxicity, since its total content in two
samples (0.48% and 0.60%) exceeded the 0.25% threshold for
a specific category of substances. For HP14, ecotoxicity tests
confirmed CB ecotoxicity, but conversely, a calculation meth-
od applied did not classify CB as ecotoxic. Nevertheless, as
the authors explain, “results of tests are to prevail”, and there-
fore, CBs are also considered toxic according to this specific
regulatory requirement.
Chevalier et al. ( 2018) addressed the dangers of CB litter,
but from a nanoscopic scale. Considering a previous study that
showed air transfer of soot nanoparticles from partial combus-
tion of cigarettes into human pulmonary system, the authors
imagined that such type of nano-litter could be present in CBs,
and contaminate leachates as well. One intrinsic characteristic
of nanoparticles is their easiness to adsorb chemicals, such as
metals and organic contaminants. This happens because of
their small size and high surface area. In addition, due to its
dimensions, it is a type of contaminant that is not easily
contained by physical barriers. Using a mixture of 10 ml of
synthetic rainwater and four CBs per vial, samples were put in
a rotator-stirring machine under certain conditions for seven
full days. After appropriate filtering and concentration of
retentates, samples of both smoked and unsmoked CB leach-
ates were analysed via different high-resolution techniques to
measure important parameters such as size distribution, aggre-
gation information, and fractal dimension. Total metal and
organic carbon percentages were determined for these solu-
tions. After conducting an ultra-filtering process, the authors
found that a number of contaminant chemical elements are
mostly present in the nano-scale particles than dissolved in
the solution; this means that a simple chemical analysis of a
filtered solution might not be representative of the real con-
tamination potential of such a solution. Therefore, it is clear
that the threats regarding nano-litter particles from CBs are of
utmost importance and must be further studied in the future.
Micevska et al. (2006) were amongst the pioneers to eval-
uate the toxicity of CBs to marine life forms. Two species
were subjected to different CB leachate solutions, with varied
brands, tar content, etc. Results indicated the acute toxicity
that CB impairs to these organisms. In fact, concentrations
as low as 25.9 mg/l and 832 mg/l were proven to be toxic to
the cladoceran Ceriodaphnia cf. dubia and bacterium Vibrio
fischeri.

Slaughter et al. ( 2011) were the first to study toxicity as-
pects of CB leachate samples to vertebrates—in this case, fish,
which are ecologically important organisms, often considered
indicators of healthy aquatic biomes. A marine species
(topsmelt—Atherinops affinis) and a freshwater species (fat-

subjects in this study. In addition, different sample conditions
were considered —smoked cigarette butts (smoked filter +
remnant tobacco), smoked cigarette filters (no tobacco), and
unsmoked cigarette filters (no tobacco). Leachates were pre-
pared to concentrations ranging from 0.125 to 16 CBs per litre
of solution, which was either dilution water (for fresh water
testing) or natural seawater. The LC50—the concentration of
CBs that causes 50% mortality—was calculated for each con-
dition considering 96 h. As expected, smoked CBs showed the
highest mortality rates, because of toxic chemicals present in
remaining tobacco. In this condition, the LC50 was roughly 1
CB/l of solution. By removing this remnant tobacco from the
smoked CB, LC50 increased to 1.8 CBs/l (topsmelt) and 4.3
CBs/l (fathead minnow); finally, the unsmoked CB resulted in
a LC50 of 5.1 CBs/l (topsmelt) and 13.5 CBs/l for the fathead
minnow. Comparing these values with previous mortality
rates reported in the literature, the authors show that species
such as Ceriodaphnia cf. dubia and Daphnia magna (aquatic
invertebrates) are more susceptible to tobacco and CB toxicity
than the tested fishes, with toxicity apparent at 0.08 to 0.25
CBs/l.
Lee and Lee (2015) found corresponding results when ver-
ifying the developmental toxicity of CB leachates in rice fish
embryos (Oryzias latipes). While low concentrations of leach-
ates from unsmoked and smoked cigarette tobacco, as well as
smoked filters, caused raised heart rate, accelerated develop-
ment, and changed behaviour, high concentrations caused re-
duction in heart rate, suppression of development, and in-
creased mortality. Testing with unsmoked cigarette filters
showed no effect, even at the highest concentration, also sug-
gesting that CB toxicity derives from tobacco plant itself and
from the by-products of its combustion.
Lawal and Ologundudu (2013) conducted another compre-
hensive study about CB leachate toxicity. They observed the
lethality of both smoked and unsmoked CBs to two aquatic
species: the western dwarf clawed frog ( Hymenochirus
curtipes) and the North African catfish ( Clarias gariepinus).
Standard 10 g/l solutions were prepared and allowed to rest for
24 h. After filtering, CB leachate solutions had their
physicochemical properties evaluated, such as temperature,
pH, and heavy metal concentration. For the bioassay, re-
searchers produced five effluent concentrations from the orig-
inal solutions, ranging from 6.25 to 100% of stock solution
concentration. Mortality rates were verified after 24 h, 48 h, 72
h, and 96 h of exposure. Results from physicochemical anal-
yses showed significant contents of heavy metals in both so-
lutions, higher than the Federal Environmental Protection
Agency (FEPA) acceptable limits for effluents for all tested
elements, except cadmium and copper. Characteristics as
odour, colour, dissolved oxygen, and pH range were found
to be also inappropriate. The authors reported significant dif-
ferences between the mortality of unsmoked and smoked CB
leachates. Indeed, after 24-h and 48-h exposure, smoked CBs
were found to be six times more toxic than their unsmoked
counterpart; this difference dropped to 2.5 times more toxicity
at the end of the experiment (96-h exposure).
In 2011, Dieng et al. ( 2011) studied the response of mos-
quito larvae (Aedes albopictus) to the presence of CBs regard-
ing ovipositing (the laying of eggs), larval eclosion, and de-
velopment. Results indicated that females did not avoid CB-
rich habitats to lay their eggs in comparison to the control
environment, but CB presence heavily influenced the mortal-
ity of young individuals (in early larvae development stages).
These findings indicated the potential use of CB as vector
control material, since it would attract the deposition of eggs
and kill young larvae. Indeed, a 2013 article published by the
same group of researchers (Dieng et al. 2013) addressed this
question, as will be discussed further in this review paper.
Molluscs that dwell in aquatic environments may also be
affected by CB leachate toxicity. Booth et al. (2015) evaluated
the impacts of CB leachate on three different species of tide
pool snails, i.e. snails that live near the shore and remain for a
significant part of their life in tide pools. A CB leachate solu-
tion was produced by placing five CBs per litre of seawater,
and letting it soak for 2 h. After removing all particles from the
solution, it was then diluted at 5%, 10%, 25%, and 100%
concentrations. The authors observed behaviour and activity
of the snails after different time intervals. Results showed
100% mortality of all species after 8-day exposure at 100%
leachate concentration (5 CBs/l), confirming the lethal toxicity
of CB leachates. Overall, when compared to other studies
involving aquatic life forms, these snails seemed to have more
resistance to CB toxicity. Exposure to lower concentrations, or
shorter time intervals, exhibited variance in response of each
species of snail activity and behaviour. This difference in sus-
ceptibility among snail species indicates that high CB pollu-
tion may alter community dynamics by differentially affecting
certain prey, and thereby predator-prey interactions.
Interestingly, snails with terrestrial habits seem to suffer
less from CB toxicity, as described in a paper by Gill et al.
(2018). In their first experiment, soil habitats moistened with
leachate solutions of equivalent CB concentrations ranging

Research by Wright et al. (2015) about the harmful effects

authors wanted to evaluate how toxic CBs could be to the

CB littering can affect natural behaviours of species that
live or adapted to live in urban areas, such as birds, for exam-
ple. Birds sometimes incorpor ate unusual materials in their
nests, including man-made materials, often with no apparent
reason (Igic et al. 2009). These materials may act as defence
mechanisms, to repel potential parasites or to stimulate the
offspring ’s immune system. As highlighted in the work of
Suárez-Rodríguez et al. (2013 ), there is evidence that
smoked CBs are becoming one such defence mechanism.
The cocktail of chemicals trapped in smoked CBs is actually
an advantage in this case, because it can affect distinct para-
sites that are already resistant to traditional chemical prod-
ucts. Using heat traps to attract common bird ectoparasites,
the authors found that the trap containing smoked CBs was
less accessed than the trap with unsmoked CBs, presumably
due to nicotine presence. In this sense, it is shown that incor-
porating CBs into nests attends one of the conditions neces-
sary to be classified as self-defence mechanism: it is delete-
rious to parasites. Later, Suárez-Rodríguez and Garcia
(2014) found a positive correlation between the CB propor-
tional usage in nest building and hatching success, fledging
success, and chick immune resp onse. The findings indicate a
positive effect from CB addition to the nests, but signs of
genotoxicity also increased when more butt cellulose was
present in the nest. Recently, Suárez-Rodríguez and Garcia
(2017) identified that the addition of cigarette butts in these
birds’ nests occur as a response to the presence of ectopara-
sites, and not simply because of a resemblance between CBs
and feathers.
Mammals are not free from CB contamination. According
to a study by Cardoso et al. ( 2018), water contamination in-
volving cigarette residues can alter the anti-predator behaviour
of female Swiss albino mice. This is an important character-
istic (anti-predator response) because it relates directly to the
central nervous system, since survival when facing a predator
is a primitive instinct. Leachate solutions were prepared at two
different nicotine concentrations, one considered an environ-
mental level (1.9 μg/l) and one as a concentrated solution (a
thousand times more concentrated than the environmental lev-
el). Animals were fed these solutions for 60 days, and their
behaviour was tested in various ways, starting on day 55.
These tests included an open-field test, a locomotion test in-
duced by a light source; olfactory and auditory tests; and,
finally, their response to the presence of predators (snakes
and cats). Overall, the presence of nicotine did not influence
eating or drinking behaviour of test subjects, and no visible
signs of intoxication were found. Locomotor activity and au-
ditory and olfactory senses, which are all simultaneously in-
volved in a predator-response situation, were also found to be
unaffected in individual tests. However, when considering the
presence of a predator, responses varied within sample groups.
Animals subjected to nicotine intoxication remained for
shorter times in the safe area and had higher frequency upright
and higher frequency freezing, evidencing an impaired re-
sponse to a predator. The authors did not observe a dose-
dependent relation, meaning that even at low concentrations,
nicotine can affect negatively structures associated with fear
responses, at biochemical or molecular level.

well as the correct disposal of these electronic devices.

Cigarette butt recycling solutions
Clearly, CB disposal habits and collection/cleaning proce-
dures are crucial to reduce potential toxic contamination of
environments, and subsequent harm to a myriad of living spe-
cies. Nevertheless, if value could be attributed to this litter
item, an additional layer of interest would be included which
could improve actions towards an ideal scenario of CB dis-
posal and collection. Table 2 reports the different proposed
applications for CBs related in this work.
Scientific literature contains a number of propositions to
reuse CBs, either in the “as-collected” condition without any
pre-treatment or after some kind of treatment. Perhaps, the
most direct use of smoked CBs is to produce cellulose acetate,
a possibility described by Benavente et al. ( 2018). Similar
research was conducted by Hemamalini et al. (2019), in which
cellulose acetate nanofibrous mat was obtained from cigarette
filter waste. The following subsections will further discuss
other proposed applications of CBs.
Biocide
An interesting application was proposed by Dieng et al.
(2013), in an article aimed at verifying the influence of CB
chemicals in Aedes aegypti, a vector of dengue. Using leach-
ate solutions obtained with deionised water and varying con-
tents of smoked CBs leaching for 24 h, the authors studied the
mortality rates of A. aegypti in different stages of larvae evo-
lution. A control sample was also produced, having no
smoked CBs in the solution. A significant relation between
the quantity of smoked CBs in the solution and mortality
effect for all larvae development stages was determined. In
addition, younger larvae were found to be more sensitive to
the presence of toxic elements, for all concentrations tested.
Overall, the results showed that CBs have potential to serve as
source material for insecticide production aimed at controlling
Aedes aegypti. Issues that the authors considered material for
future studies, at the time, were the potential geno- and cyto-
toxic effects on humans that these solutions may have as well
as their acceptability in regional communities. Interestingly,
they also consider that, if decomposing CBs are shown to have
the same potential, ashtrays that act as lethal A. aegypti traps
could be produced.
One year later, the same research group published another
study (Dieng et al. 2014) concerning the influence of CB
toxicity on diverse behaviours of adult vectors of Aedes
aegypti . They found that oviposition and larval eclosion
rates were not significantly reduced in substrates containing
CB leachates. However, results showed reduced fecundity of
female adults that developed as larvae in a habitat containing
CB leachates, as well as reduced lifespan when compared to
control groups. Later on, Murugan et al. ( 2018) applied this
concept to malaria vectors, showing that solutions originated
from CBs showed highly toxic properties to young instars and
adults of Anopheles stephensi, but did not alter the predatory
efficiency of Mesocyclops aspericornis(a predator of mosqui-
to larvae).
Corrosion inhibition
Zhao et al. ( 2010a) are among the first researchers to turn to
CBs for value, investigating the potential of CB leachate so-
lutions as corrosion inhibitors in N80 steel, a material used in
the oil extraction industry. Using a solution consisting of five
CBs in 100 ml of distilled water, aged for 24 h, the authors
determined the presence of compounds that act in favour of
metal corrosion inhibition, by mechanisms described in the
original article. Overall, inhibition action relates to the con-
centration of cigarette butts in water extracts. For a 5 CBs/l
solution, maximum inhibition efficiencies of 94.6% and
91.7% in acidified 10% and 15% HCl solutions were found.
Additional studies by the same research group found that aer-
obic CB water extracts are more efficient than anaerobic so-
lutions (Zhao et al. 2010b), and that CuCl addition promotes a
synergistic effect to recycled CBs in inhibiting this specific
corrosion scenario (Zhang et al. 2011).

weight, via rolling in a two-roll mill according to ASTM stan-
dards. The authors found that the addition of an optimal 4% in
weight of cigarette butts to the material improved mechanical
characteristics such as tensile strength, tear strength and max-

Mohajerani et al. ( 2016) envisioned a possible solution
involving the civil construction, a branch of industry that re-
lates to voluminous amounts of supplies. To study the feasi-
bility of incorporating CBs into fired clay bricks, the authors
evaluated the influence of distinct variables on mechanical
properties of the bricks —CB content, mixing time, and
heating time. They found that the addition of CBs in the quan-
tities proposed (2.5%, 5%, 7.5%, and 10% in weight percent-
age) resulted in reduced values of dry density and compressive
strength, such that bricks containing more than 2.5% CBs
would not be feasible for construction. However, samples
containing 2.5% CBs presented only a slight reduction of
mechanical properties, attaining requirements for constructing
buildings. Ultimately, the authors theorise that the addition of
1% CBs in weight would represent a practical solution to the
CB litter problem, since the reduction in mechanical proper-
ties would be minimal. Trough simple calculations, they reach
the conclusion that the brick production worldwide is so mas-
sive that if only 2.5% of the brick production could incorpo-
rate 1% in weight of CBs, all of the littered CBs globally
would be used. Indeed, in 2020, a new publication by the same
group (Kurmus and Mohajerani 2020) investigating CB addi-
tions in percent weight from 0.5 to 2% confirmed this
hypothesis.
Wadalkar et al. ( 2018) published work also considering
CBs as fillers for the construction industry, but in precast
concrete blocks. They found that the addition of 0.1% CBs
in weight does not cause significant reduction in mechanical
properties of these blocks, and therefore, this application
could represent a practical solution to use CB litter. Another
construction material that can be used with CB waste is gyp-
sum, as reported by Morales-Segura et al. ( 2020). Results
indicated suitability of use in applications where bonding to
a ceramic support is not needed, requiring high surface hard-
ness and/or mechanical resistance.
Asphalt concrete (AC) for pavement construction could
also incorporate CBs, as reported by Mohajerani et al.
(2017). This use would also benefit from the large-scale
amounts involved with automotive transportation. In order to
avoid toxicity issues associated with chemical translocation of
toxic materials present in the cigarette butts, the authors per-
formed an encapsulation step, prior to AC mixing, consisting
of soaking the material in an encapsulating medium. The au-
thors present the results of two studies: firstly, they evaluated
the effects of incorporating CBs encapsulated with three dis-
tinct classes of bitumen (C170, C320, and C660), in different
proportions (10 kg/m 3, 15 kg/m 3, and 25 kg/m 3), into AC
mixes manufactured with one specific class of bitumen
(C170). Afterwards, the authors assessed the influence of
adding 10 kg/m3 of CBs encapsulated with paraffin wax into
AC mixes produced with two distinct classes of bitumen
(C170 and C320). In the first study, findings showed that
AC fabricated with 10 kg/m3 or 15 kg/m3 CBs yielded accept-
able properties, such as flow, stability, resilient modulus, bulk
density, and percentage of air voids. CBs encapsulated with
higher grade bitumen generated ACs with greater bulk density
and maximum density, as expected, and the addition of CBs in
larger quantities decreases the general density of AC, since
CBs replace heavier aggregates in the mix. The second
study proved that the addition of 10 kg/m 3 of paraffin
encapsulated CBs in both C170 and C320 AC mixes is
viable and yields properties acceptable for light traffic
requirements, which would be a possible destination for
CBs. More recently, Rahman et al. ( 2020) evaluated the ap-
plication of CBs in bitumen as a replacement for virgin cellu-
lose fibre as fibre modifier, with very interesting results.
Electric material and batteries
In the field of electric applications, CBs demonstrate interest-
ing possibilities. For example, good conductive material can
be obtained by a simple heat treatment process (Ghosh et al.
2017). Hussein et al. ( 2017) considered CBs potential filler
material for a PVC cement resin composite, which is a sub-
stance with adhesive properties commonly used to “weld”
pieces of plastic together. Commonly used as insulators in
voltage engineering, these composites may have their dielec-
tric permittivity and electrical conductivity tuned through in-
corporation of conductive fillers, such as CBs. For different
filler concentrations (0.1%, 0.2%, 0.3%, 0.4%, and 0.5%), the
authors studied the material response in terms of dielectric
properties when altering frequency range and temperature
range. They found that the permittivity increased as filler con-
tent and/or temperature increased, due to effects of interfacial
polarisation (filler content) and segmental mobility of polymer
molecules (temperature); higher frequencies, however,
yielded lower values of permittivity. Also, the composite
showed lower values of electrical impedance with higher
values of filler content, temperature, or frequency.
Examples of research involving batteries are those of Yu
et al. (2018) and Wang et al. ( 2018), who described recovery
of CBs for producing anode materials in lithium-ion batteries,
and Xu et al. ( 2019) who produced hierarchically porous
carbon@graphene composites derived from carbonised CBs
applicable as cathode hosts for lithium-sulphur batteries.
Earlier on, Huang et al. ( 2015) had proposed to use recycled
cigarette filters in preparing membranes to act as separators in
lithium-ion batteries.
Supercapacitors
Wang et al. ( 2016) proposed the application of used cigarette
filters as parent material for obtaining carbon fibre/vanadium
nitride precursors for supercapacitors. Initially, CBs were

Li et al. ( 2020) reported the production of a functional

supercapacitors and adsorbents, in which the properties of
the material were shown to be strongly affected by the
activation process parameters. Using a different processing
method, called hydrothermal synthesis, Ubaidullah et al.

electrochemical performance in supercapacitors as well as ex-

Lee et al. ( 2014) used smoked cigarette filters to obtain
nitrogen-doped meso/microporous hybrid carbon material.
Results showed better rate capability and higher specific ca-
pacitance for the supercapacitor when compared to conven-
tional activated carbons; a similar work with promising results
was published more recently by Meng et al. ( 2019).
Nanomaterials
Ogundare et al. (2017) studied the feasibility of using CBs as
source material for nanocrystalline cellulose, a promising ma-
terial that presents interesting properties such as high strength,
high surface area, low density, low toxicity, and biocompati-
bility. For this reason, sources for its production have recently
included recycled materials. In their work, from discarded
CBs, the authors managed to isolate nanocrystalline cellulose.
The results of their characterisation were promising, and trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed needle-like cel-
lulose nanocrystals measuring 143 nm in length and 8 nm in
width, in average, which represents an interesting aspect ratio
for use as reinforcement. The overall nanocellulose yield from
CBs was 29.4%, which is more than two times the value
obtained from old corrugated paper (13.3%) and roughly 1.5
times the value obtained for wastepaper (19.0%). It was con-
cluded that this material is feasible for nanocrystalline cellu-
lose production, if there is an appropriate collection logistics
by consumers.
Carbon dots, a new emerging category of nanomaterials,
have been evaluated as potential outputs of CB litter research.
Bandi et al. ( 2018) reported the conversion of toxic CBs to
carbon dots, which showed a number of potential applications,
including security ink, bioimaging, and sensing, while Anmei
et al. ( 2018) envisioned the application of carbon quantum
dots obtained from cigarette filters in fluorescence detection
of an important carcinogenic food colourant.
Sound absorption
Acoustical isolation could take advantage of fibrous and po-
rous structures of cellulose acetate, the main component of
cigarette filters. Porosity plays a very important role in acous-
tic properties, and these samples could profit from a “double
porosity”: the inner porosity of the CBs, as well as the porosity
resulting from air gaps between packed CBs (Escobar and
Maderuelo-Sanz 2017). Following ISO 10534-2:1998 stan-
dard, the authors evaluated the acoustical performance of sam-
ples prepared by simply placing the CBs inside impedance
tubes appropriate for this kind of measurement, without using
binders. Different types of CBs were tested (smoked CBs,
unused CBs manually separated from the cigarette, and pur-
chased CBs), as well as different packing conditions (10 to 14
butts packed inside the tube). When compared to commercial
sound-absorbing materials, samples prepared with CBs
showed superior acoustic absorption at mid frequencies (from
500 to 4000 Hz). Thus, the fabrication of novel sound-
absorbing materials from CBs is promising.

ducted further studies regarding sound absorption. This time,
researchers conducted a pre-treatment of CBs consisting of

Energy and related topics
Kim et al. ( 2016) envisioned the potential of CBs to function
as a supporting material for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR).
In fuel cells, electrocatalysts have the function of accelerating
the ORR, and the traditionally used material is platinum due to
its high activity. However, factors such as high cost and lim-
ited storage encourage research towards reducing platinum
use and enhancing catalytic activity of platinum. Supporting
materials with high surface area, adequate pore structure, and
good conductivity, such as carbon materials, are used to pro-
mote this catalytic enhancement by permitting high dispersion
of platinum nanoparticles. In their work, carbon materials
were produced from used cigarette filters, after separation
and heat treatments at 900 °C for 2 h, in two conditions: N
atmosphere (N-doped carbon) and Ar atmosphere (without
doping). Then, platinum dispersion was conducted by the
conventional impregnation method. The advantages of this
simple route are the recycling of CBs as carbon source for
platinum supports, the development of a bimodal structure

(pores in different scales —mesopores and micropores), and
simultaneous introduction of N functional groups (in the case
of N-doped carbon), which are important to the formation of
homogeneous nucleation sites for platinum nanoparticles.
Following an extensive set of characterisation methods, it
was found that high surface area, bimodal pore size distribu-
tion, and N doping contributed to excellent results in terms of
platinum dispersion—in fact, dispersion was higher than that
of commercially used carbon support material.
Blankenship and Mokaya ( 2017) published a study
proposing CBs as source for u ltra-high surface area car-
bons destined for hydrogen intake. Hydrogen is being
researched as an energy source material because of two
main characteristics: its gravimetric energy capacity, and
the fact that this material does not generate CO 2 emis-
sions when used for energy production. Naturally, there
is also a driving need for materials capable of storing
this hydrogen, and materials with high surface area (a
common trait of porous materials) can store considerable
amounts via physisorption. The authors found an inter-
esting relation concerning smoked CBs and the effect of
activation temperature on their porosity. Usually, higher
activation temperatures yield higher porosities and sur-
face areas, and this trend was observed in unsmoked
CBs. For smoked CBs, however, optimal porosity and
surface area properties were encountered at an interme-
diate activation temperatu re, and higher temperatures
yielded lower results. The explanation for this anomaly,
according to the authors, is the presence of metal addi-
tives (such as K, Ca, Na, and Mg) as remains from the
tobacco combustion process. Working as activation sites,
they sum up to the activation effect caused by KOH and
cause this odd behaviour. Exceptional results of hydro-
gen storage were reported in this article —in fact, the
results are presented as “highest hydrogen uptake capac-
ity reported to date for any carbons or porous materials
in general ”, combining the ecological effects of clean
fuel emissions, waste disposal, and valorisation of re-
sources in a perfect manner.
In 2020, Wang et al. addressed the production of ester-rich
bio-oil from used cigarette filters via microwave-assisted py-
rolysis process, and they estimated that over 350,000 MT of
this bio-oil can be produced if the technology used in the
process could be scaled up worldwide (Wang et al. 2020).
An application which could contribute twofold to a cleaner
environment is the utilisation of CBs to obtain phototermal
conversion material, as described by Sun et al. ( 2019). In this
scenario, material produced from CBs and coated with re-
duced graphene oxide is able to absorb sunlight and convert
it into heat, which in turn will generate clean steam energy;
indeed, the authors reported energy conversion efficiency of
94% in conditions similar to those practical in solar energy
generation.
Chemical adsorption
Hamzah and Umar ( 2017) proposed the fabrication of active
carbons from cigarette filter waste, using KOH chemical acti-
vation induced by microwave power input. Surface area and
methylene blue adsorption capacity were evaluated with
promising results. Lima et al. (2018) also studied the potential
of CBs for the adsorption of pollutants, describing a sponta-
neous methylene blue adsorption process happening in
hydrochars fabricated from CBs. The potential for activated
carbon production may even be enhanced by pre-swelling of
CBs, raising specific surface area up to 47%, as explained by
Koochaki et al. (2020). Different approaches related to degra-
dation of pollutants in water include a study where CBs were
used as source for a superfast flow reactor (Xie et al. 2020),
and an article describing the fabrication of a niobium carbide/
carbon nanocomposite to be used as photocatalyst in which
CBs were the carbon source (Gupta and Pandey 2018).
Efforts for CB valorisation have been made also in the drug
and pharmaceutical industry. For example, Bilge et al. (2019)
recently published an article in which toxic CBs demonstrated
potential to become sensor material for sensitive determina-
tion of an antihypertensive drug. In 2016, Nascimento et al.
(2016) investigated the potential application of cigarette filters
in a composite designed to work as an adsorbent of phenyl-
butazone (PBZ), a commonly used anti-inflammatory drug.
This pharmaceutical ends up in the environment, and long-
term exposure to it may result in adverse effects to humans, as
well as wildlife. Besides CBs, this composite also contains
polypyrrole (PPy) and polyaniline (Pani), two polymers that
as a composite have properties such as hydrophobicity, acid-
base character, and polar functional groups. The composite
was produced in a single-step triple-phase interfacial reaction,
in a temperature from 0 to 5 °C. This simple fabrication pro-
cess contributes to the feasibility of using the material as an
adsorbent. Additionally, properties such as high surface area,
heterogeneous surface, and modest porosity are of interest
and, indeed, resulted in a maximum adsorption capacity of
100.22 mg g−1—a result that indicates promising future appli-
cations in the separation field.
Other examples of drug removal applications include that
by Abu-Danso et al. (2019) of using processed cellulose from
CBs to absorb diclofenac from water and Zhang et al. (2020b),
who produced an interesting CB-based activated carbon ma-
terial of high surface area to remove ciprofloxacin and sodium
dodecyl sulphate from aqueous solutions.
The potential as an absorbent medium was also investigated
for the disposal of wastewater generated in nuclear plants, which
contain radionuclide uranium (Pu et al. 2019). The adsorption
capacity surpassed that of untreated activated carbon and showed
a very interesting adsorption selectivity. Earlier, Sun et al. (2017)
presented interesting results concerning the adsorption capacity
of CBs, after KOH activation, for CO 2 and iodine vapour

are the preparation of materials for phenol adsorption (Chen et al.
2015), removal of lead from aqueous solution using charred
carbon derived from used cigarette filters (Soltani et al.2015),
and selective adsorption of tobacco-specific nitrosamines in so-

Electromagnetic wave absorption
Interestingly, electromagnetic wave absorption is another field
where recycled cigarette filters may be useful, as published by
Wang et al. (2015). Electromagnetic waves are a considerable
source of interference and radiation, mostly from wireless
technology, digital devices, and radar systems. The authors
obtained graphene aerogel composites from CB litter, which
showed good results —further enhanced by a polypyrrole
coating.
Pulp production
One great example of a complete process for CB recycling is
the production of cellulose pulp for paper industry, using CBs
and alkaline pulping (Teixeira et al. 2017). Samples prepared
with CBs showed satisfactory mechanical properties for use,
and a low degree of whiteness that may require additional
bleaching depending on the final use of the paper. The by-
product of this process is a dark liquor generated from cooking
the mixture, which showed eventual presence of aldehydes
and other carbonylated compounds, nitrosamines, nicotine,
and heavy metals. Under Brazilian law, this by-product could
be discarded directly in a water body without further treat-
ment, even if compounds such as nitrosamines and nicotine
were detected. This fact occurs because current Brazilian laws
do not establish limits for these specific chemicals.
Nevertheless, the authors studied possible means of removing
this toxicity. They devised a way of incorporating this clari-
fied effluent repeatedly into new cooking batches, as part of
the mixture. As explained in the article, the whole process was
licensed to a recycling company, which is producing good-
quality pulp for a cardboard factory. Since the effluent is
reused in the process, this is an example of a complete cycle
that effectively contributes to clean our environment of CBs.
Conclusion
Environmental hazard results from reckless improper disposal of
toxic waste, and cigarette butts are proven in literature to be
source of damage to a variety of living species, from invertebrate
diminutive aquatic species to adult rodents, even if present in
considerably small concentrations. The knowledge about its con-
tamination power is paramount to enlighten smokers and en-
hance public efforts aimed at raising worldwide awareness re-
garding this topic. While the toxic effects of CBs to simpler
organisms are more present in literature, there seems to be a
gap in relation to research concerning long-term effects of CB
toxicity to mammals, indicating a possible future research trend.
Considering the variety of possibilities for recycling ciga-
rette butts described in this article, ranging from applications
with zero to low value addition (aimed mostly at getting rid of
CBs) to high-value end products derived from litter, it is pos-
sible to foresee a future where careless dumping of cigarette
filters in the environment will be minimised. Applications
related to chemical adsorption, energy production, and crea-
tion of nanomaterials seem to be excellent options that add
value to this litter and therefore must be further investigated,
while the incorporation of CBs in civil construction is inter-
esting considering the significant amount of CBs that could be
used. In addition, the search for new applications must con-
tinue since the creation of value is the key factor to transform
cigarette butts from an unwanted persistent litter to a desired
valuable raw material.
