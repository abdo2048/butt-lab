# Method family F1 — mechanical separation and aqueous washing

**Batch-02 desk synthesis. Date: 2026-11-16. Audience: medicine-faculty students and interested general readers. Source-grounded desk review only — not an operating procedure, plant design, safety approval, or legal opinion. No experiment was performed. No claim that this method is clean, safe, validated, or environmentally beneficial.**

## Overview

Cigarette-butt filters are mostly cellulose acetate (CA), a plastic-like polymer, bound together with a little paper and tobacco residue and held in the mouth-end by a tipping paper. The mechanical/physical family separates the plastic filter from the rest of the cigarette waste by grinding, then washes the separated material with water to remove surface contaminants, dries it, and mills it into fine particles. Companies in this family (e.g., MéGo! in France) melt and compress the cleaned fibre into sheets sold for products such as urban furniture.

No source located in the batch-02 evidence base establishes that washing removes nicotine, metals, or other cigarette contaminants from the fibre to any stated level. The strongest independent environmental assessment found (a claimed MéGo! ACV reporting 88% CO₂ reduction) is not publicly available.

## Mechanism

Butts arrive as collected whole units. A mill grinds them so the plastic filter separates from the tobacco and paper matrix. Sieves catch the filter fraction; the paper/tobacco/ash fraction is rejected as a separate stream. The filter fraction enters a water-wash stage (some designs use two wash tanks in sequence), where water carries away surface dust, loose tobacco, and dissolved contaminants. Wash water may be recycled. The wet acetate is dried at elevated temperature, then milled again into fine fibrous particles. Some operators melt and thermo-compress the dried particles into solid sheets.

> **[GAP-CLOSING NOTE — AI, scientist-inventor role]** No source in the batch-02 corpus states the particle size of the ground filter, the wash temperature, contact time, water-to-feed ratio, or the final dried-product purity. The grinding step likely releases inhalable cellulose-acetate dust and nicotine-laden particles; no source reports measured dust concentrations or worker-exposure controls. These are evidence gaps, not assumed values.

## Feedstock requirements

Collected whole cigarette butts. Companies report collection through dedicated ashtrays (e.g., MéGo!'s EcoAction Plus network). One company-stated split (Capbiotek, quoting Ouest-France 2017) is approximately 1 kg per 2,000 butts, divided as 12% paper/tobacco/ash, 8% sludge, 80% cellulose acetate. An Ouest-France 2018 figure of 4,000 butts/kg conflicts with this; the basis (wet vs. dry mass, filter-only vs. whole-butt) is not stated by either source. A 2019–2022 company statement reports 35 tonnes treated with a target of 200 tonnes/year by 2025; capacity figures are self-reported and not independently verified.

> **[GAP-CLOSING NOTE — AI, scientist-inventor role]** No source states whether collected butts must be dry, whether filters are separated from the tobacco rod before grinding, or whether the 12/8/80 split was measured on a dry-mass basis. Without a stated basis, per-1,000-butt mass figures (e.g., 0.40 kg CA per 1,000 butts) cannot be compared across families.

## Contaminant fate

Cigarette butts carry nicotine, tobacco-specific nitrosamines (TSNAs), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and metals (Cd, Pb, As, Hg) from tobacco, paper, and filter additives. Washing partitions these between three streams: the fibre, the wash water, and the rejected solids/sludge. No source located reports analyte-specific removal data for any of these classes. A closed water loop without a measured purge is a concentration loop — contaminants accumulate in the recycled water rather than leaving the system. The rejected 12% paper/tobacco/ash stream and the 8% sludge stream are candidate contaminant sinks, but neither has a demonstrated final disposal route, and it is not established whether sludge is incorporated into the final product.

> **[GAP-CLOSING NOTE — AI, scientist-inventor role]** No source states the fate of metals in this family. If sludge containing concentrated metals is melted into thermo-compressed sheets, the product may carry a concentrated metal burden unless metals are separately removed and disposed of. Metals are an explicit stop condition for this workflow: no metals-removal outcome or lawful metals sink is demonstrated for F1.

These are company claims, not measured operating data. No source reports energy per tonne of collected butts, water consumption per cycle after recycle, or the treatment technology inside the water recycler.

Company claims that sludge is "dried, melted, and thermo-compressed into product" mean the 8% sludge fraction may remain inside the sold product unless separately demonstrated otherwise.

Cigarette-butt input is classified as dangerous waste under INERIS waste classification (HP6/HP14). No source located states an End-of-Waste determination, product standard, or regulatory approval for F1 output in any jurisdiction. Re-Cig's End-of-Waste annex references UNI 10667 specifications but does not disclose per-lot testing or a process LCA.

## Bottom line

F1 is the most conventional cigarette-butt recycling route (mechanical separation plus water washing, no exotic solvent) and therefore the most comparable baseline across families. Its scientific weak point is shared by every wash route: partitioning — whether washing removes contaminants from the fibre or merely redistributes them between fibre, water, and sludge. No source demonstrates that the fibre/product retains contaminants below any justified criterion, or that the water loop has a defined purge and disposal route. Metals remain an unresolved stop condition. No experiment was performed; no safety, clean, validated, or environmental-benefit claim is made.
