## Keywords:

Plastic wastes Cigarette butts Microplastics Nanoplastics Leachates Circular economy

Cigarette butts constitute the most ubiquitous plastic litter worldwide, serving as a persistent source of microplastic fibers and toxic chemical leachates in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems. Primarily made of cellulose acetate, discarded butts degrade slowly via physicochemical processes, liberating cigarette butt-derived microplastics alongside hazardous additives such as nicotine, heavy metals, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. This PRISMA-guided narrative review consolidates evidence on the environmental prevalence, transport, and fate of these microplastics, rigorously appraises progress in analytical detection methods, and evaluates their ecotoxicological impacts across food web levels. Studies reveal induction of behavioral changes, elevated mortality, and bioaccumulation in aquatic species, with particles functioning as carriers for persistent organic contaminants and metals. The review also addresses the considerable economic costs of cigarette butt pollution and scrutinizes mitigation options, including cutting-edge removal techniques, extended producer responsibility schemes, and circular-economy pathways such as cellulose acetate reclamation and composite sorbent fabrication. Persistent gaps exist in long-term degradation patterns, realistic exposure limits, and practical remediation efficacy, necessitating focused research to support evidence-based policies and sustainable waste management.

## 1. Introduction

Global solid waste generation is projected to increase significantly, with annual production expected to rise from 2.01 billion tonnes in 2016 to 3.40 billion tonnes by 2050, according to the World Bank (Oladipupo et al., 2024; Yogaswara et al., 2024). This growth is driven by population growth, urbanization, increased tourism, and changing consumption patterns, all of which contribute to higher volumes of litter entering terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems (Oladipupo et al., 2024; Yogaswara et al., 2024; Yousef et al., 2024). CBs are the most highly generated waste globally. Furthermore, specifically, cigarette butts (CBs) have been identified as a major contributor to the menace of plastic pollution, leading to environmental contamination, with around 4.5 trillion CBs being discarded annually (Valiente et al., 2020; Slaughter et al., 2011). In 2016, global cigarette production reached 5.7 trillion, predominantly composed of cellulose acetate (CA), a non-biodegradable synthetic

polymer that releases microplastics (CBMPs) and toxic leachates into ecosystems (Yousef et al., 2024; Díaz-Mendoza et al., 2024; Venugopal et al., 2021). CBs are not only a source of microplastic fibers but also release over 7000 toxic substances, including nicotine, a neurotoxic chemical that disrupts neurotransmitter release in the autonomic and central nervous systems (Valiente et al., 2020; Slaughter et al., 2011; Díaz-Mendoza et al., 2024; Venugopal et al., 2021). Prolonged activation of nicotinic acetylcholinergic receptors (nAChRs) may lead to receptor desensitisation, impairing brain function (Wright et al., 2015). Additionally, at least 250 harmful compounds, including 69 carcinogens, are released as CBs degrade, contaminating soil and water systems (Zeng et al., 2023), (Ajibade et al., 2023). For example, discarded cigarette butts can contribute to water contamination, with smoked filters showing higher toxicity than unsmoked filters owing to the accumulation of harmful substances during smoking (Green et al., 2014), (Slaughter et al., 2011; Belzagui et al., 2021). The ecological and health impacts of CB pollution are profound.

Besides affecting wildlife, CB contamination poses indirect risks to human health by consuming contaminated crops or animals (Yang et al., 2024; Green et al., 2020). Furthermore, male birds that stayed longer in the nest, caring for the chicks, were found to have suffered genotoxic damage, indicating that prolonged exposure to these compounds poses significant risks (Suárez-Rodríguez et al., 2017). It is known that aquatic species, such as fish, turtles, whales, and birds, often ingest CBs, which can disrupt their digestive systems and lead to poisoning or starvation (Oceancare; Novotny et al., 2011). ## 2. Methodology

This review adopts a PRISMA-guided narrative synthesis approach to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and broad coverage of the literature, while retaining flexibility appropriate for a multidisciplinary environmental review. The methodology was designed to systematically identify, screen, and synthesise peer-reviewed studies on cigarette butt–derived microplastics (CBMPs), associated leachates, ecotoxicological impacts, analytical characterisation methods, and circular-economy-based mitigation strategies.

### 2.1. Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple scientific databases to minimise selection bias and maximise coverage. The primary databases consulted were Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, and Google Scholar, covering the period January 2015 to February 05, 2026. This time window was selected to capture both early foundational studies on cigarette butt pollution. Search strings were adapted to the syntax and indexing structure of each database while preserving the same core conceptual framework (pollution source, plastic characteristics, and environmental impact). Minor variations in keywords were necessary to optimise retrieval efficiency and relevance across platforms, particularly for Google Scholar, which has limitations on complex Boolean queries. Google Scholar returned approximately 1610 search results. Consistent with established practice for systematic and scoping environmental reviews, only the first 300 relevance-ranked records were screened, as results beyond this threshold showed diminishing relevance and high redundancy. Google Scholar's ranking algorithm prioritizes citation relevance, and screening a defined subset improves feasibility while maintaining coverage of the most influential literature. Full texts were evaluated systematically, and studies not meeting the eligibility criteria were excluded with documented reasons. ### 2.4. Data extraction and thematic synthesis

From each included study, the following information was extracted.

• Publication year and geographic focus • Environmental matrix (water, sediment, soil, biota) • Type of cigarette butt material (smoked, unsmoked, biodegradable) • Microplastic characteristics (size, morphology, polymer type) • Analytical techniques employed (e.g., SEM, FTIR, ICP-MS) • Ecotoxicological endpoints and test organisms • Mitigation, recycling, or circular economy applications

Studies with methodological limitations were not excluded but were explicitly contextualised in the discussion to avoid over-interpretation. This approach is consistent with best practice for narrative environmental reviews and allows integration of emerging evidence in a rapidly evolving research field.

## 3. Global burden and source of CBMPs

Improperly disposed of CBs release toxic compounds, polluting terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems. Studies have shown that these discarded filters leach hazardous chemicals, presenting substantial threats to ecological integrity and human health.

The European Waste Framework Directive regards CBs as hazardous waste. More specifically, they belong to the HP 6 (toxicity) and HP 14 (ecotoxicity) groups (Rebischung et al., 2018a). Their nicotine concentration and the harmful chemicals they release into the environment

define this classification mostly (Zeng et al., 2023; Rebischung et al., 2018a). The full classification is presented in Table 4, along with brief highlights. ### 3.2. Biodegradable CBs: environmental burdens and misconceptions

Biodegradable cigarette filters have recently been suggested as alternatives to traditional cellulose acetate (CA) filters to address environmental concerns. Historically, cigarette filters were first introduced between 1860 and 1920 to prevent tobacco particles from entering the smoker's mouth (George et al., 2022). Cellulose acetate filters have been commercially available since 1927, originating with filter-tipped cigarettes in Switzerland (Pauly et al., 2009). Following the early 1950s ‘lung cancer scare', cigarette manufacturers heavily invested in filter-tipped cigarette design and marketing (Zipser, 1954). Consumers widely perceived these 'filters' as reducing harmful smoke constituents, with Congressman John Blatnik noting in 1957 that the term “filter' carried substantial preconceived efficacy (Harris, 2011). This public trust created a critical window for tobacco engineers to develop genuinely effective filtration, though industry scientists soon recognized the inherent challenges, coining the term “filter problem' by 1954 (Klamkin, 1957). Despite these efforts, modern cellulose acetate filters, now ubiquitous, proved largely ineffective at reducing health risks and posed persistent environmental issues due to their slow degradation in natural settings (Vert et al., 2012). According to the European Commission's standards (EN 13432), true biodegradability requires compostable materials to convert at least 90% into CO2 under microbial action within six months, a threshold unmet by conventional cigarette filters (Innocenti and Biodegradability, 2018). Therefore, this characteristic feature is validated with the aid of a series of standardized laboratory test procedures (Koster, 2025). This has, however, generated heated debate about the biodegradability of CBs, which is due to the conducted assessment under controlled laboratory conditions and differs from the complex real-world environmental representations (Vert et al., 2012; Koster, 2025). The actual degradation of CBs occurs through a combination of biodegradation and photodegradation processes (Koster, 2025). It is important to emphasize that the ecotoxicological impact of biodegradable filters is complex and of concern. For instance, a recent study by Koroleva and colleagues found that although both biodegradable and non-biodegradable CBs contain different heavy metals, biodegradable CBs more significantly altered bacterial community composition. This was attributed to their higher levels of leached hazardous metals (Koroleva et al., 2021). The authors further employed automated ribosomal intergenic spacer analysis (ARISA) for the evaluation of bacterial diversity before and during CB leachate exposure. The reported findings indicated that biodegradable CBs significantly altered the architecture of bacterial communities more than non-biodegradable ones. These findings further highlight the detrimental effects of CB pollution on microbial communities, which are critical for maintaining soil health. Consequently, the inference drawn from their study confirmed that biodegradable CBs may not be as environmentally friendly as previously thought (Koroleva et al., 2021).

This misconception may also lead to increased pollution due to a false sense of environmental safety. Smokers are likely to believe these filters are harmless, making them more likely to be disposed of inappropriately. Therefore, biodegradable cigarette filters may not be the answer they initially appear to be. The persistence of hazardous chemicals, the potential for increased CB waste, the formation of CBMPs, and the lack of clarity surrounding the ecotoxicological impacts suggest that these alternatives may result in new environmental issues. ## 4. Environmental fate and impacts of CB-MPs

### 4.1. Potential generation of microplastic fibers from CBs

Cigarette filters are designed to capture harmful compounds and particles found in tobacco smoke and are commonly made from cellulose acetate (CA). The continuous release of microplastic fibers from CBs into the environment can be triggered by physical, chemical, and biological breakdown processes (Hon, 1977; Ach, 1993). A recent study examined how microfibers (MFs) generated from cigarette-smoked filters (SFs) contribute to MPs pollution. According to their study, synthetic fibers containing 15,000 strands release around 100 microfibers (MFs) smaller than 0.2 mm daily. An estimated 0.3 million tons of microfibers could be added to aquatic environments each year as a result of this phenomenon. Additionally, the authors established that MFs in SF leachate demonstrated high toxicity to *Daphnia magna*, with EC50 and EC100 values of 0.017 SF/L and 0.620 SF/L, respectively. Their study clarified the high frequency of synthetic polymers found in deep-sea sediments by showing that microfibers made of synthetic materials constitute a significant source of total MPs (Belzagui et al., 2021).

Another study conducted in Dalian, China, examined 35 distinct cigarette brands and 10,591 CBs. The analysis estimated that Chinese CBs contribute around 360,000 tonnes of plastic waste yearly. Recently, the discharge and contamination levels of MFs were examined across four Chinese cities. CB contamination varied, with Guangzhou having 0.03 ± 0.02 CBs/m<sup>2</sup>, Meizhou 0.07 ± 0.02 CBs/m<sup>2</sup>, Baoding 0.06 ± 0.02 CBs/m<sup>2</sup>, and Dalian 0.10 ± 0.03 CBs/m<sup>2</sup>. ICP-MS detected 629.7 μg/L of 14 heavy metals, while SEM and EDS investigations showed the presence of MPs and heavy metal particles in CBs, with an estimated 1.9 ± 0.9 g/km<sup>2</sup>/day leaking into the environment (Yang et al., 2024). The vector potential of CB-derived MPs has also been reported recently. In one study, CA fibers from cigarette filters were also examined as sources of trace metals in coastal waterways. The fibers with release rates ranging from 40% to 90% were observed by the authors. These results highlight the environmental role of cellulosic MFs in transporting metals in aquatic systems (Turner et al., 2024).

Generally, MPs can be produced by both smoked and unsmoked cigarette filters (Acarer Arat, 2024). Researchers milled MPs from smoked and unsmoked cigarette filters to mimic environmental generation, which was evaluated to quantify their toxic effects on a few chosen aquatic and terrestrial species. After 48 h of exposure, *D. magna* (100 mg L<sup>−1</sup> of SCF-MPs) and *B. calyciflorus* (10 mg L<sup>−1</sup>) showed significantly reduced survival, with SCF-MPs exhibiting more severe effects than UCF-MPs. Heavy metal levels were also assessed in both UCF-MPs and SCF-MPs (Fig. 3). Their research indicates the need for efficient environmental control to reduce CB pollution from both smoked and unsmoked cigarettes (Dolar et al., 2025).

### 4.2. Associated chemical contaminants: heavy metals and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)

Beyond their role as microplastic sources, cigarette butts (CBs) act as vectors for chemical contaminants, including heavy metals (e.g., cadmium, lead, arsenic) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) (Yousef et al., 2024; Dobaradaran et al., 2020). These compounds originate from both the tobacco and additives in filters, and can leach into soil, sediments, and aquatic systems upon disposal. Leachate studies

indicate that heavy metals and PAHs are readily mobilized under environmental conditions, potentially impacting microbial communities and higher trophic levels (Yousef et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). While these contaminants are distinct from the microplastic fraction, their co-occurrence underscores the multifaceted environmental hazard posed by CBs, necessitating integrated management strategies that address both physical and chemical pollution pathways (Acarer Arat, 2024). In a recent study, heavy metal analysis revealed that the most contaminated brand contained 17 metals, while the least contaminated brand contained 15, with concentrations ranging from 0.08 mg/L to 297 mg/L. Nine PAHs were detected in both brands, with concentrations ranging from 0.40 ng/L to 92.12 ng/L (Yang et al., 2023a). The ageing effects on CA obtained from CBs were investigated in a separate study. The authors confirmed that CA fragmentation into MPs is attributed to the ageing effect. Furthermore, CBs also pollute soil and marine ecosystems by releasing toxic substances such as nicotine, arsenic, and heavy metals, as reported by the authors (Muˇsiˇc et al., 2023). Recently, the discharge and contamination levels of MFs were examined across four Chinese cities. CB contamination varied, with Guangzhou having 0.03 ± 0.02 CBs/m<sup>2</sup>, Meizhou 0.07 ± 0.02 CBs/m<sup>2</sup>, Baoding 0.06 ± 0.02 CBs/m<sup>2</sup>, and Dalian 0.10 ± 0.03 CBs/m<sup>2</sup>. ICP-MS detected 629.7 μg/L of 14 heavy metals, while SEM and EDS investigations showed the presence of MPs and heavy metal particles in CBs, with an estimated 1.9 ± 0.9 g/km<sup>2</sup>/day leaking into the environment (Yang et al., 2024). The vector potential of CB-derived MPs has also been reported recently. In one study, CA fibers from cigarette filters were also examined as sources of trace metals in coastal waterways. The fibers with release rates ranging from 40% to 90% were observed by the authors (Turner et al., 2024). These results highlight the environmental role of cellulosic MFs in transporting metals in aquatic systems (Acarer, 2023).

#### 4.2.1. Evidence-based human exposure routes to CBMPs

Humans are exposed to microfibers and associated chemicals primarily through ingestion and inhalation, with secondary pathways including dermal contact (Yang et al., 2023b; Savva et al., 2023). Ingestion occurs via contaminated seafood such as fish and bivalves, where microfibers from degraded filters accumulate through aquatic food webs (Novotny et al., 2011; Dolar et al., 2025). Drinking water further contributes to oral exposure, as microfibers persist in urban runoff and wastewater (Hecht et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023b). Inhalation represents a major route in indoor and outdoor settings, with airborne cellulose acetate fibers from littered butts likely penetrating the lungs (Yang et al., 2023b; Talhout et al., 2024). Particles as small as a few micrometres can translocate into the bloodstream and tissues (Yang et al., 2023b; Everaert et al., 2023). Dermal exposure, though less quantified, occurs through direct contact with littered CBs in public spaces, potentially leading to incidental ingestion (Araújo et al., 2022; Rochman et al., 2013). While systemic detection of microplastics in human placenta, lungs, and blood confirms ubiquitous exposure, no studies have specifically quantified CBMP intake, despite their prevalence as the most littered plastic item globally, plausibly amplifying total microplastic burden across these pathways.

#### 4.2.2. Cigarette butt specific additives and leachate exposure

Leachates from CBs can release nicotine, heavy metals such as lead, cadmium, and chromium, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and aromatic amines, all of which are recognized toxicants with documented human health effects at relevant doses (Yousef et al., 2024; Booth et al., 2015; Dobaradaran et al., 2020). The cellulose acetate fibers themselves are prevalent in the environment and can act as vectors for sorbed chemical contaminants, which may be mobilized through weathering (Novotny et al., 2011; El Hadri et al., 2021). While direct measurements of CB-derived microplastics in human tissues remain limited, the combination of physical particles and associated chemical load underscores the plausibility of cigarette butts as a contributor to human microplastic exposure beyond the background environmental

#### 4.2.3. Evidence quality and uncertainty

Despite established pathways of exposure, causal links between CBMP exposure and human health outcomes remain uncertain due to sparse epidemiological data. Epidemiological evidence is currently limited, with systematic reviews suggesting possible impacts on digestive, respiratory, and reproductive health, but the overall quality of human data is low (Zeng et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2025; Choudhary et al., 2024). Direct evidence quantifying human intake of CB-derived microplastics or leachate chemicals is currently scarce, representing a significant knowledge gap. Regulatory and methodological challenges, including variability in analytical methods, detection limits, and the lack of standardized exposure metrics, further complicate comprehensive health risk assessment.

### 4.3. The hydrological implications of CBMPs

Hydrological studies indicate that human activity and land use patterns affect the generation and distribution of CBMPs (Suárez-Rodríguez et al., 2017; El Hadri et al., 2021). Discarded CBs release MPs through multiple environmental processes (Ajibade et al., 2023; Belzagui et al., 2021; Green et al., 2020). Due to their size, intact cigarette filters are initially impeded from infiltrating deeper soil layers (Mohamed, 2023; Ajibade et al., 2024). However, once broken down into micro- or nanoparticles, CBMPs can migrate across soil matrices, especially via macropores, fractures, or root channels (Turner et al., 2024; Mohamed, 2023). The hydrophobicity and surface charge of CBMPs typically influence their interactions with mineral surfaces and colloids, which subsequently influence their environmental mobility or retention (Turner et al., 2024; Mohamed, 2023; Ajibade et al., 2024). CBMPs negatively impact soil texture, organic matter content, and pore water ionic strength (Mohamed, 2023; Ajibade et al., 2024).

Moreover, littering of CBs in the urban environment is a major environmental threat to the quality of urban water, owing to contamination from various hazardous chemicals contained in them, such as the rapid release of heavy metals and nicotine (Yang et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2021). Studies have demonstrated that during peak discharge measures, the flux of floating plastics, including CBMPs, can increase several-fold, highlighting the pivotal role of hydrology in plastic transport dynamics

(Yang et al., 2024; Turner et al., 2024).

Urban and recreational areas, as well as sites near mining and agricultural operations, tend to exhibit higher concentrations of MPs and associated contaminants (Yogaswara et al., 2024; Díaz-Mendoza et al., 2024; Turner et al., 2024). Cumulative upstream contributions and lower dilution capacity often raise contaminant levels in downstream rivers and lakes (Novotny et al., 2009; Shen et al., 2021). Geographic variation emphasizes the need for a hydrological framework when assessing MP fate and mobility from CBs (Novotny et al., 2009; Michielssen et al., 2016). Once CBMPs find their way into aquatic bodies, owing to their buoyancy and tiny size, they cluster in sediments or remain in the water column, depending on hydrodynamic conditions (Wright et al., 2015; Belzagui et al., 2021). Estuaries and deltas are particularly vulnerable, as tidal pumping and flow variations prolong MP residence time and increase exposure risks (Freire Lima et al., 2021). CBs can also serve as sources and sinks for other environmental contaminants due to their high sorption capacity (El Hadri et al., 2021). According to research, 43.9–93% of fish and marine zooplankton contain microplastic fibers from CBs, which allow for bioaccumulation through trophic transmission (Shen et al., 2021). This leads to disruptions in osmoregulation, reproduction, and predator avoidance behaviors among aquatic organisms, threatening ecosystem stability (Yadav et al., 2024; Biefel et al., 2024). Given the prevalence of CB pollution and its transformation into MPs, hydrological risk evaluations and pollution mitigation measures must be re-examined. Understanding the transport mechanisms and the environmental fate of CB-MPs is crucial for developing effective solutions to safeguard water quality, aquatic biodiversity, and public health.

### 4.4. The fate of CBMPs in aquatic ecosystems

Complex systems comprising transport, deposition, transformation, and degradation define the fate of CBMPs in aquatic environments (Yang et al., 2024; Hon, 1977). Cigarette filters, primarily made of CA (Joly et al., 2018; Hon, 1977), are a major source of MPs entering aquatic systems through direct littering, wind movement, and urban runoff due to improper disposal. Upon entering aquatic habitats, CBMPs are subjected to hydrodynamic pressures, causing them to disperse throughout sediments, the water column, and surface waters (Freire Lima et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2025). ### 4.5. CBMPs as vectors for contaminant exposure

MPs generated from CBs have demonstrated a strong capacity to act as vectors for various environmental contaminants (Turner et al., 2024; Rochman et al., 2013). MFs generated from the smoked filters (SFs) of CBs, for instance, might serve as vectors for a range of hazardous substances (Desideri et al., 2019). Cigarette filters act as partial barriers that trap chemicals during smoking; however, once discarded, they release these substances into the environment (Green et al., 2020). Whereas some chemicals leach slowly over time, others are rapidly released, posing immediate threats to both soil and aquatic ecosystems (Green et al., 2020; Muˇsiˇc et al., 2023). Separate studies have found that the leachate produced by burning cigarette filters has high Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) values, ranging from 2100 to 11,300 mg/L (Hossaini Motlagh et al., 2024).

Studies show that cigarettes leave behind almost 7000 chemicals, including heavy metals, nicotine, and PAHs, a large proportion of which have mutagenic or carcinogenic effects (Zeng et al., 2023). One of the major concerns is the adsorption of persistent organic contaminants (POPs) onto MPs produced from cigarettes. These MPs can act as concentrated reservoirs of harmful chemicals, often accumulating them in quantities much higher than those in the ambient water. For instance, a class of flame retardants called polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs) has been found to gather on MPs and then be passed on to aquatic life upon consumption (Sun et al., 2019). For example, research on Japanese medaka (*Oryzias latipes*) found that fish exposed to marine plastic particles had significantly higher PBDE levels than the control groups (Rochman et al., 2013).

This suggests that CBMPs can also play a vector role for PBDEs or similar contaminants in aquatic organisms. Furthermore, CBMPs have been found to adsorb heavy metals like iron and zinc, as revealed through atomic absorption spectroscopy analysis of CB leachates (Díaz-Mendoza et al., 2024). ### 4.6. CB-derived leachate: toxicity and implications for aquatic organisms

CB leachates have been associated with significant metal pollution

and phytotoxicity in both coastal and aquatic environments (Venugopal et al., 2021; Garrido Lazo et al., 2024). Given that CBs are the most commonly found garbage on beaches worldwide, their inappropriate disposal introduces various toxic chemicals into aquatic ecosystems (Shen et al., 2021). Leachates from CBs pose both acute and chronic risks to aquatic life (Sharma et al., 2025). The toxicity of the CB is determined by its form; the most hazardous are smoked cigarettes (SCB) containing tobacco residue, followed by smoked filters (SF) devoid of tobacco and unsmoked filters (USF). Lethal concentration (LC50) for freshwater and marine fish species varies, with SCBs proving harmful at concentrations as low as one butt per litre (Slaughter et al., 2011).

Numerous aquatic organisms are impacted by the effluent from CBs, including fish, algae, and invertebrates, particularly water fleas, which include *Daphnia magna* and *Ceriodaphnia* cf *dubia*, and marine bacteria like *Vibrio fischeri*. (Slaughter et al., 2011; Venugopal et al., 2021). In stagnant water bodies, like ponds and slowly flowing streams, CBs can release dangerous substances over extended periods, significantly impacting the aquatic ecosystem (Slaughter et al., 2011; Sharma et al., 2025). To safeguard aquatic environments, it is imperative to develop efficient waste management to address CB pollution. Recent discoveries of microplastic fibers emitted by CBs have also revealed increased ecotoxicity to aquatic species, such as fish, mollusks, and copepods (Yang et al., 2023a; Garrido Lazo et al., 2024). Additionally, research has demonstrated that leachates from CBs have detrimental impacts on fish populations, highlighting the need for comprehensive environmental risk assessments (Sharma et al., 2025; Garrido Lazo et al., 2024).

## 5. Analysis and characterisation of CBMPs

The characterisation of MPs generated from CBs requires a versatile approach including several advanced analytical techniques, as detailed below.

### 5.1. SEM

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) has been the main tool used to study the morphological features of MPs obtained from CBs. For example, in a recent study examining the inflammatory effects of MPs from CBs on peripheral blood mononucleated cells, 2000x-resolution images analyzed with ImageJ were used to determine average fibre diameters. This was achieved utilizing measurements made at several points along several fibres visible in the SEM pictures. Furthermore, studies have shown variations in fibre diameters both inside individual filters and between brands. High-resolution SEM imaging has also revealed the complex architecture of CA fibres found in cigarette filters (Soltani et al., 2025).

Using SEM, the surface properties and internal structure of untreated (control) CA, smoked cigarette filters, and intermediate-stage biodegradable filters were investigated in another study. By means of a concentrated electron beam, this sophisticated microscopy approach generates high-resolution images of the surface of the filter, therefore facilitating the assessment and comparison of structural changes occurring during the degradation process (Parida et al., 2025). Each CB reportedly releases about 100 small microfibers (measured less than 0.2 mm) daily, as earlier published (Belzagui et al., 2021). Highly detailed photographs of surface features revealed by the SEM confirmed that MPs

were generated from CBs.

### 5.2. IR (FTIR and TG-FTIR)

The pyrolysis behaviour of mixed MPs, particularly those generated from CBs, has been investigated using thermogravimetric analysis in concert with Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (TG-FTIR). This approach offers important new perspectives on the thermal degradation behaviour of these materials and the emission of volatile chemicals during degradation. Thus, using TG-FTIR, the kinetics of pyrolysis have been investigated together with the chemical components liberated during thermal degradation (Yousef et al., 2024). Usually, the acquired crucial data tends to assist in developing suitable mitigation approaches, waste management, and recycling plans for these microplastic fibers from CBs. Moreover, by tracking the development of several functional groups using FTIR spectra at several phases of the pyrolysis process, researchers can now gain new insights of the properties of the produced volatile molecules and the fundamental processes of degradation mechanisms. Especially in relation to cellulose-based MPs, TG-FTIR can thus clarify the thermal stability of cellulose acetate and the generation of possibly harmful compounds during its decomposition (Yousef et al., 2024; Gr¯ınbergs et al., 2021). Moreover, the technique can help to detect the microfibers generated by cigarette remnants, a major source. Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier-Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy has proven its usefulness as a robust approach for the characterisation of the chemical composition of CBMPs (Yogaswara et al., 2024).

ATR-FTIR spectrometers are known for their ease of use and minimal sample preparation, making them suitable for routine investigation of environmental samples (Belzagui et al., 2021; De Cesaris et al., 2024). Characteristic bands linked with the acetyl group at wavenumbers of 1240, 1370, and 1750 cm<sup>−1</sup> have been shown to reflect the cellulose acetate composition present in tobacco filters (Yogaswara et al., 2024). This method has also been applied to examine the breakdown of CA in the discarded CBs, therefore offering important details on the environmental permanence of these materials. The non-destructive feature of ATR-FTIR makes it particularly helpful for tracking changes in the chemical structure of CB MPs over time (Araújo et al., 2022). Furthermore, ATR-FTIR can identify MPs smaller than 20 μm, and has demonstrated sensitivity to particles as small as 6 μm, making it especially useful for studying minute MPs from cigarette filters (Othman et al., 2023).

### 5.3. EDS

In a recent study, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used for elemental analysis, confirming the presence of heavy metals on sample surfaces. For more precise quantification, Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) was also employed, revealing a total heavy metal concentration of 629.7 μg/L across 14 elements. Leachate analysis from cigarette butts have uncovered several poisonous compounds, including anthracene (0.40 ng/L) and naphthalene (92.12 ng/L) (Yang et al., 2024).

### 5.4. HPLC-MS/MS

High-Performance Liquid Chromatography combined with Mass Spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS) has been used to detect PAHs in cigarette butt leachates. These results highlight the possible environmental risks posed by harmful chemicals leaching from MPs generated by CBs (Yang et al., 2023a).

### 5.5. Zeta potential

Zeta potential measurements have been employed to understand the surface charge characteristics of cigarette butt-derived MPs. A study

investigating the fate of nanoplastics in the environment reported that polystyrene-based nanoplastics with similar zeta potentials (−45 to −40 mV), but different sizes (50-800 nm) and morphologies, showed considerable sorption capacities when interacting with cigarette filters (El Hadri et al., 2021). This finding highlights the potential role of CBs in the transport and accumulation of other types of microplastics in the environment.

### 5.6. XPS and Raman spectroscopy

Emerging techniques such as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Raman spectroscopy can provide detailed information on the molecular architecture and surface chemistry of CBMPs (Othman et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2020). These methods could provide supplementary information to FTIR and EDS, allowing for a more complete characterisation of the complex nature of CB-derived contaminants.

### 5.7. GPC

Gel Permeation Chromatography (GPC) has also been applied to detect cellulose acetate remaining in composted CB samples, revealing insights into the biodegradation process of cigarette filters (Parida et al., 2022; Alkhoury et al., 2024). GPC analysis has confirmed the absolute lack of CA peaks in the composted samples, indicating the biodegradation under controlled conditions (Parida et al., 2022). This useful knowledge is crucial for devising effective solutions to ameliorate the environmental impacts of MPs generated from CBs and for influencing legislative decisions designed to minimise cigarette butt pollution.

## 6. Economic implications

The economic implications of cigarette plastic waste on a global scale are substantial, amounting to approximately US$26 billion, which includes US$20.7 billion attributed to damage to marine ecosystems and US$5 billion allocated for waste management (Sy, 2024). The financial burden of addressing CB-related litter falls heavily on taxpayers and has broad implications for national economies. For instance, in China, the annual cost is approximately US$2.6 billion; in India, it exceeds US$760 million; in Brazil, it surpasses US$200 million; in Germany, it is greater than US$230 million; and in Australia, it amounts to A$73 million (Green et al., 2023; CU, 2019). Moreover, research conducted in 2020 revealed the financial cost implications of cleaning up cigarette butt in the thirty largest US cities. The authors reported annual expenses ranging from US$4.7 million to US$90 million per city, with an average per capita cost estimated at US$6.46. Collectively, these thirty cities spend nearly US$264.5 million annually on CB clean-up (Schneider et al., 2020).

CB pollution imposes economic costs beyond the United States. In the United Kingdom, projected clean-up expenses reached £40 million (US $55 million)(UK, 2021). France allocates about €100 million (US$115 million) annually to manage CB-related waste (MacKenzie et al., 2021). These numbers underline the significant financial burden accrued on different countries. Beyond only direct clean-up and disposal expenses, cigarette butt pollution has additional economic consequences. For example, in Australia, discarded cigarettes were estimated to have caused 4558 fires in 2014-2015, resulting in damages of $80.8 million. Excluding bushfire-related costs, a 15-year Australian study (2003–2017), found that smoking materials, including cigarettes and pipes, accounted for 18% (161out of 900) of home fire-related deaths (Coates et al., 2019). Similarly, the 2012 Alt Empordà wildfires in Catalonia, Spain, highlight the severe consequences of improperly discarded CBs. With almost 13,800 ha of land burnt, this terrible event caused significant environmental and financial damage as well as four deaths. Investigations found that the main source of these fires was improperly discarded CBs (Anderson, 2024).

## 7. Sustainable solutions to manage cigarette butts

Recent climate change, partially attributable to plastic waste, coupled with growing public awareness, necessitates a shift from a linear plastic economy to a more sustainable and circular model of management (Oladipupo et al., 2024; Choudhary et al., 2024). In this paradigm, the recycling and upcycling of plastic waste are imperative. Consequently, determining effective strategies to accomplish this objective represents one of the principal challenges (Oladipupo et al., 2024). CB wastes remain the most ubiquitous and recognized form of plastic pollution globally, posing considerable environmental and health hazards (Laurenza et al., 2021). Therefore, as awareness of their impact increases, novel solutions are required to tackle this widespread issue. Sustainable strategies need to be designed to alleviate environmental

degradation and avert microplastic generation and contamination from CBs. Given this, we hereby present and outline significant innovative and sustainable solutions below.

### 7.1. Cellulose acetate recovery and composite sorbents

As knowledge of the environmental effects of these ubiquitous contaminants rises, mitigating techniques for controlling CBMPs become ever more crucial. To prevent generation of MPs and add value to waste, a recent study developed a new, green, and effective extraction technique to produce CA fibres from CBs. The approach involved washing CBs in hot water at 50 °C for 60 min, followed by multiple rinses in cold water and ethanol. Afterward, the resultant CA fibres were then dried at 60 °C for 1 h (De Fenzo et al., 2020). This approach not only mitigates contaminants but also maintains the purity of the obtained CA. The authors affirmed that the fabricated CA looks similar to pure CA fibres used in cigarette filters and could address CB degradation and leakage into the environment. Another study innovatively employed the recovered CA as a precursor platform for synthesizing of a versatile adsorbent subsequently used in water treatment. After using hot water and ethanol for cleaning the filters, porous cylinder-shaped crystals were produced. With removal yields above 79.6%, these cryogels augmented with activated carbon, were applied remove organic contaminants from water (De Cesaris et al., 2024).

This approach not only reduces the environmental impact of CBs but also offers a means to address water contamination. Several analytical approaches, including UV and ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, TGA, and DSC, confirmed the efficiency of this process (De Cesaris et al., 2024). Using a phase inversion technique, another study sustainably recycled CBs and filters into CA membranes. With removal rates of 85–90%, the resultant membranes demonstrated great efficiency in extracting heavy metals (chromium, cadmium, and lead) from aqueous solutions. This method not only produces valuable materials for water treatment but also presents a viable solution for CB pollution (Torkashvand et al., 2022).

### 7.2. Biorefinery: a potential innovative solution to CBs

Although much research has focused on policy and public sensitisation regarding improper disposal and its environmental consequences, implementing innovative solutions is necessary. As nations grapple with the pervasive impact of CBs, new approaches offer sustainable, environmentally benign solutions to turn this waste stream into value-added resources. Therefore, this section focuses on innovative approaches to transform CBs into valuable materials. Biorefinery offers a novel method to solve the persistent problem of cigarette butt pollution, generate value-added materials, and support the transition toward a circular economy and materials revolution, all while prioritizing environmental cleanliness. For instance, a recent study has investigated the potential of biorefinery systems to generate fermentable sugars and bioethanol, providing a sustainable pathway to turn hazardous waste into value-added material towards the materials revolution (Battista et al., 2023).

Another study subjected the spent CBs to various pretreatment approaches to obtain CA fibre. The obtained fibres were then enzymatically treated to produce fermentable sugars. The authors further indicated that adding cellulase at 10% of the CBs concentration yielded promising results. During microbial fermentation, these fermentable sugars served as a viable substrate for bioethanol production. This eco-friendly approach not only produces bioethanol, useful in biofuel and chemical industries, but also helps mitigate CB pollution through the biorefinery process (see Fig. 7) (Battista et al., 2023).

A recent study also used CBs as a cellulosic precursor in a biorefinery process employing a hydrothermal strategy with phosphoric acid as a catalyst to produce levulinic acid, a key precursor in the production of biofuel (Laurenza et al., 2021). The approach has also been extended to generate triacetin from CBs via pyrolysis. This is another way of addressing limited resources, as wastes are being converted into value-added materials (Yousef et al., 2023). Furthermore, with the aid of pyrolysis or gasification, syngas, bio-oil, and biochar have also been reportedly generated from CBs (Wang et al., 2020). Among the benefits of using biorefinery systems are their scalability and integration into existing waste management frameworks. Establishing specialised facilities for processing CBs tends to help in building a sustainable loop capable of turning these hazardous wastes into useful resources. In valorising waste, this reduces environmental load and landfill pressure while opening financial opportunities (Adeleye et al., 2023).

As studies and pilot projects continue to demonstrate feasibility, biorefinery presents a sustainable and practical solution to global cigarette butt pollution. (Battista et al., 2023). Beyond diverting CBs from waterways and landfills, their collection and processing could generate financial incentives. Developing scalable, cost-effective technologies for CB valorising is crucial to advancing the circular economy and materials recovery efforts.

### 7.3. Integrated solutions for the environmental impacts of CBMPs

While policy interventions and public awareness are essential in dealing with the CBMPs, developing integrated techniques with sustainable features is equally important. Both individual and combined remediation techniques are necessary. Various techniques have been successfully employed for the removal of MPs in aquatic environments. Therefore, various removal methods, such as the membrane bioreactor (MBR) (Michielssen et al., 2016), adsorption (Adeleye et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023), advanced oxidation processes (John et al., 2023), coagulation (Park et al., 2021), photocatalysis (John et al., 2023), and filtration (Akarsu et al., 2021) have been explored to mitigate MPs pollution. Among these, adsorption is considered a cost-effective method (Adeleye et al., 2025). For example, one study achieved 99.96% removal efficiency using magnetically modified biochar from rice husks, attributed to surface complexation and electrostatic interaction (Wu et al., 2023). ### 7.4. Extended producer responsibility (EPR): key effective strategy for cigarette butt waste management

EPR is widely acknowledged as a mechanism to ensure industries bear responsibility for their environmental impacts. Fundamentally, EPR schemes place end-of-life product management obligations on producers, often internalizing environmental externalities into product costs (Croes et al., 2023). At its core, an EPR scheme assigns producers the responsibility for managing their products at the end of their lifecycle, in some instances, with the effect of internalizing negative environmental externalities into the product's price (Diggle et al., 2020; Novotny et al., 2014). Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) represents one of the most effective policy instruments for addressing cigarette butt (CB) and tobacco-related waste pollution, making producers accountable for environmental impacts across the full product lifecycle, including end-of-life management (Croes et al., 2023; Novotny et al., 2014). In the US bill, Federal lawmakers in February 2020 introduced the Break Free From Plastic Pollution Act. ## 8. Comparative evaluation of mitigation strategies of cigarette butts and key trade-offs

### 8.1. Comparative evaluation

To translate mitigation concepts into actionable frameworks, we developed a decision matrix that evaluates key cigarette butt (CB) mitigation and circular economy options across six criteria: Technology Readiness Level (TRL), cost, scalability, emissions, secondary pollution risk, and social acceptance (Table 6). These criteria reflect not only environmental performance but also practical deployment considerations necessary for high-value policy and industry decision-making. EPR and regulatory bans tend to have the highest potential to reduce environmental inputs and are relatively low-cost to implement once mandated, but require robust governance and industry cooperation (Diggle et al., 2020; Sy et al., 2025). Collection and recycling/upcycling offer circular solutions, but economic feasibility and market demand for recovered materials remain key constraints. For example, recovered cellulose acetate is useable in membranes and fashion products, though quality and processing cost must be balanced (Torkashvand et al., 2022). Biorefinery and pyrolysis can valorize CBs into fuels or chemicals, but often involve high energy consumption and may generate secondary emissions if not carefully designed and regulated (George et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2020). Advanced sorbent materials and composites offer novel pathways for microplastic removal, but current development is largely at pilot or lab scale and requires further work for field deployment (Adeleye et al., 2025). By evaluating these technologies through a consistent lens, we provide readers with a clearer understanding of feasibility, risks, and potential trade-offs associated with each mitigation pathway. This framework supports balanced decision-making and

identifies areas where further research and investment are needed.

### 8.2. Key trade-offs

Collection programs and public litter management are well-established and socially acceptable, but their effectiveness depends heavily on community participation and sustained funding (Zafeiridou et al., 2018). EPR schemes offer a policy-driven approach to internalize environmental costs and incentivize product redesign, but they carry risks of tobacco industry greenwashing unless safeguards are implemented, as highlighted in the EU and UN treaty contexts (Green et al., 2023). Bans and tobacco control policies remain the most effective in reducing CB generation at source and provide co-benefits for public health (Sy, 2024; Green et al., 2023). Recycling, upcycling, and sorbent-based removal offer promising circular economy and remediation pathways, but they are currently limited by technological readiness, cost, and potential secondary pollution if microplastics are not fully contained (Adeleye et al., 2025; Torkashvand et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2020). Pyrolysis and biorefinery approaches can recover energy or chemical feedstocks but are capital-intensive and not yet scalable (Jankovi´c et al., 2023; Premchand et al., 2025). Overall, an integrated strategy combining upstream regulatory measures (bans, EPR) with targeted downstream interventions (collection, recycling, remediation) is likely the most effective pathway for reducing CB pollution while balancing feasibility, cost, and environmental outcomes.

## 10. Research roadmap: priority questions for future studies

Building on the identified gaps, the following prioritised research questions are proposed to guide future investigations and policy-relevant science.

i. **Degradation Dynamics:** *How do cellulose acetate cigarette filters*

*fragment into micro- and nanoplastics under realistic environmental* *stressors over time* (Belzagui et al., 2021; Suárez-Rodríguez et al., 2017; Croes et al., 2023)? Understanding the long-term fragmentation of cellulose acetate cigarette filters under realistic environmental stressors remains limited. Future studies should quantify micro- and nanoplastic generation under combined UV radiation, mechanical abrasion, and wet–dry cycling across seasonal to multi-year timescales to better reflect environmental exposure conditions. ii. **Chemical–Physical Interactions:** *How does environmental aging*

*influence the co-release of microplastics and sorbed toxicants from* *cigarette filters* (Venugopal et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024; Novotny et al., 2009)? Environmental aging may alter filter surface chemistry, enhancing both microplastic release and toxicant mobilisation. Critical knowledge gaps remain regarding the relative contributions of particulate-bound versus dissolved fractions of nicotine, PAHs, and metals to observed toxicity, particularly following oxidative or photochemical weathering. iii. **Smoked vs Unsmoked Filters:** *To what extent do smoked and*

*unsmoked cigarette filters differ in microplastic release, leachate* *composition, and toxicity* (Slaughter et al., 2011; Zeng et al., 2023; Soltani et al., 2025)? Smoked filters exhibit markedly different chemical loads and surface properties compared with unsmoked filters, yet their comparative microplastic release rates, leachate toxicity, and sorption behaviour remain poorly constrained.

Systematic side-by-side studies are required to disentangle combustion-derived effects from polymer aging alone.

iv. **Environmental Fate and Transport:** *What are the dominant*

*transport pathways and environmental sinks of cigarette butt*–*derived* *microplastics* (Booth et al., 2015; Oceancare; Watson et al., 2016)? The transport pathways and environmental sinks of cigarette butt-derived microplastics across soils, stormwater, rivers, and coastal systems are insufficiently resolved. Future work should integrate field observations with hydrodynamic and particle-tracking models to identify dominant accumulation zones and long-range dispersal potential. v. **Ecotoxicological Thresholds:** *What are environmentally relevant*

*exposure concentrations and toxicity thresholds for CB-derived* *microplastics and leachates* (Díaz-Mendoza et al., 2024; Green et al., 2014; Freire Lima et al., 2021)? ## 11. Conclusions

Cigarette butts are among the most widespread yet underregulated sources of plastic pollution worldwide. Composed predominantly of cellulose acetate fibers, they undergo fragmentation and weathering processes in the environment, generating cigarette butt-derived microplastics and nanoplastics that contribute significantly to secondary microplastic contamination across terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems. Transportation through urban runoff, stormwater pathways, and tidal exchange promotes their redistribution, with estuarine and coastal zones emerging as major accumulation areas. Beyond their long environmental persistence, cigarette butts represent chemically complex hazardous waste. Smoked filters leach a variety of toxic substances, including nicotine, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and heavy metals. At the same time, the associated microplastics act as multifunctional contaminant carriers capable of adsorbing and mobilizing additional contaminants in the environment.

Experimental evidence across diverse taxa has demonstrated acute and chronic toxicity at environmentally relevant concentrations, manifesting in developmental, behavioral, reproductive, and genotoxic effects. Advances in characterisation techniques have improved the understanding of their morphology, surface chemistry, interactions with contaminants, and degradation behavior. However, current methodological inconsistency limits comparability across studies and constrains the assessment of ecological risks. Development of standardized protocols for sampling, artificial weathering, and toxicity testing is necessary to improve reproducibility and support reliable environmental risk evaluation. Important knowledge gaps persist regarding long term degradation dynamics, contaminant release rates, transport modeling, human exposure pathways, and the establishment of environmentally

realistic risk thresholds. The environmental and socio-economic consequences of cigarette butt pollution are extensive, encompassing ecosystem degradation, increased waste management demands, and infrastructure maintenance costs.

Addressing these impacts requires a strategic combination of upstream and downstream measures. Preventive approaches such as product design reform, regulatory controls, and extended producer responsibility offer the highest potential for source reduction. Complementary downstream strategies, including recycling, material valorisation, and site-specific remediation, can mitigate existing contamination and support circular economy principles. Robust governance frameworks are essential to ensure regulatory effectiveness and to prevent superficial or misleading sustainability claims. Overall, cigarette butt-derived microplastics represent a distinct priority category of microplastic pollution that integrates polymer persistence, chemical hazard, and socioeconomic burden. Coordinated scientific standardization, evidence-based policy development, and preventive management strategies are crucial to mitigate their long-term environmental and societal effects.
