REVIEW ARTICLE

# A systematic review on cigarette butt management as a hazardous

waste and prevalent litter: control and recycling

Javad Torkashvand<sup>1,2,3</sup> & Mahdi Farzadkia<sup>1,2</sup>

Received: 20 October 2018 /Accepted: 14 January 2019 /Published online: 12 March 2019 # Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2019

Abstract Annually, over 5.5 trillion cigarettes are produced worldwide, and it is estimated that 4.5 trillion cigarette butts (CBs) are still being littered in the world. The dispersal of the CBs has caused this hazardous waste to be considered as one of the most important litters and environmental risks all over the world. This systematic study with the search protocol definition and keyword identification was developed to find the CBs control and recycling methods by searching in five scientific databases. Founded articles were monitored and finally, 35 related articles were selected and studied by the authors. The results of this study showed that CBs recycling methods have been tested in 10 specific categories all over the world. The CBs have been applied without any pre-processing methods in the bricks and asphalt production. However, other applications of the CBs such as the absorbent material production, vector control, and use as a biofilm carrier in wastewater treatment need various processing methods. The researchers also presented models and suggestions such as taxes, penalties, and public education for the control of CBs littering. Despite the innovative methods applied for the CBs recycling in previous studies, CBs have not received much attention in terms of pollutant control and environmental issues in recycling processes.

Considering the CB-related contaminants such as heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), formalde-hyde, acetaldehydes, hydrogen cyanide, and benzene (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012; Dieng et al. 2013; Mohajerani et al. 2016), as well as its high dispersion in the world, CBs are one of the most important wastes in the world (Ariza and Leatherman 2012; Dieng et al. 2014; Mohajerani et al. 2017; Maderuelo-Sanz et al. 2018; Murugan et al. 2018) and it is considered as a serious environmental concern due to toxic effects on living organisms (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012; Ariza and Leatherman 2012; Kadir et al. 2014; Mohajerani et al. 2016, 2017). The cigarette filter as the main part of CBs is made up of cellulose acetate, which is a photo-degradable (Sabzali et al. 2012; Dieng et al. 2013); it is con-sidered as non-biodegradable material (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012; Ariza and Leatherman 2012; Dieng et al. 2013; Kadir et al. 2014; Mohajerani et al. 2016). Mohajerani 2011; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012; Sabzali et al. 2012; Dieng et al. 2013; Kadir et al. 2014), so that it leaks toxic and chemical substances to the environment. In addition to the toxicity, CB contaminants are likely to enter the food chain (Barnes 2011; Sarani and Kadir 2013; Betânia et al. 2016; Mohajerani et al. 2016; Ou et al. 2016). Due to the difficulty and the high cost of CB collection (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Yazdi et al. 2012; Sarani and Kadir 2013; Kadir et al. 2014; Mohajerani et al. 2016) and the lack of a simple method to extract chemical and toxic substances existing in the CB (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Sarani and Kadir 2013), finding solutions to control and reduce the littered CBs seems essen-tial. Furthermore, given that CB landfilling is not recommend-ed economically and environmentally (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012; Kadir et al. 2014), and hazardous wastes incineration emission is of great concern due to the toxic pol-lutants emission in the exhaust gases (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012; Kadir et al. 2014), it is necessary to identify new methods for CBs recycling. Therefore, due to the high dispersion of CBs as a litter in the world, high CB toxicity as well as considering the complex conditions for the final dis-posal of this hazardous waste, the present study systematically reviews the researchers’ experiences on CBs recycling and control pathways.

Method

Literature search

This systematic study was carried out following the def-inition of a search protocol, identification of the valuable keywords and sources of available information. This re-view was performed by searching documents in five ma-jor scientific databases which focused on CBs as most important solid wastes. The search for papers was carried out in June 2017. The research results and strategy have been presented in Table 1.

Screening and selection criteria

The documents adopted from different databases were select-ed after removing duplicates and then screened considering the scope of the present study. The methodology to screen most relative documents is descried follows:

(I). Title and abstract: papers with a title and/or abstract which were not consistent with CBs were excluded, even if the selected keyword were found in these studies. (II). Abstract: each paper abstract was read once more to further select papers that addressed CBs. (III). Content: the full paper was read to identify the studies that cover CB recycling control methods. (IV). Related documents: relevant documents referenced on the initially selected papers were searched and read. CB recycling methods

Use of CBs in brick production

Recycling toxic wastes through integrating them into an inert component so as to create the building materials can be con-sidered as an applicable solution (Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012). Thus, CBs can be used as an additive and raw material in brick production (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Kadir et al. 2014; Mohajerani et al. 2016). Moreover, CBs in bricks can abate the leakage of some pollutants, such as heavy metals (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012), which is due to the trapping characteristics of CBs. In addition, the high-temperature com-bustion process of bricks production (1050 °C) is an effective way for metal leakage reduction due to the formation of metal oxide (Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012). Of course, the presence

of cellulose acetate, paper, and remained tobacco in CBs lead to increased pollutant emission within the heating process; it was reported that with increase of the CBs in bricks material, the emission of CO, CO2, and HCN are accordingly increased (Mohajerani et al. 2016). CB is thermal value waste (Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Kadir et al. 2014), so that it leads to energy saving in the brick production (Mohajerani et al. 2016). With increasing the ratio of CBs in bricks, the density of bricks decreased significantly; for example 10% of CBs in bricks lead to 636 kg/m<sup>3</sup> (30%) reduction in density (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011). Also, CB recycling in the bricks pro-duction can increase the porosity due to cellulose acetate burn-ing and decrease the density and heat conductivity (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Sarani and Kadir 2013; Mohajerani et al. 2016). This decreases in density and increases in porosity lead to the creation of lighter bricks that reduce transmission and installation costs (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011). However, this weight loss and in-creased porosity can lead to a decrease in the final product strength (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Kadir et al. 2014). In addition, CBs in bricks increase the water absorption capacity and subsequent-ly the shrinkage is increased with the increase in CB ratio. The thermal conductivity of bricks depends on their density and porosity, and by adding 5 and 10% CB in bricks, their thermal conductivity was reduced by 51 and 58%, respectively, which can be considered as an effective way in energy saving issues (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011). The mixing rate or the heating rate in the brick production process with different CB ratios influences the final properties (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012; Kadir et al. 2014; Mohajerani et al. 2016). The heating rate is an effective factor in brick production; in lesser heating rate, bricks with better character-istics are produced. Researchers found that 2.5% CBs by weight in the brick and the 1 °C/min heating rate are ideal conditions for brick production (Kadir et al. 2014).

Use of CBs in asphalt production

A method for CB recycling is applying it in the asphalt produc-tion (Mohajerani et al. 2017). In this sense, Mohajerani et al. (2017) encapsulated CBs in bitumen and then used it to make asphalt for the pavement. CBs were placed at 105 °C for 24 h in order to remove the trapped moisture. Then, CBs were encap-sulated in 150 °C hot pot. The study showed that when the amount of CBs is increased, the strength of the produced as-phalt was reduced, so that this characteristic should be consid-ered in asphalt standards for light to heavy traffic types. Furthermore, the increased encapsulated CB ratio in asphalt decreases its bulk density which can be different in terms of bitumen class. Eventually, the researchers integrated 10 to 15 kg CB per cubic meter of concrete to achieve acceptable properties in the asphalt production (Mohajerani et al. 2017).

Use of CBs for vector control

Various chemicals found in CBs can be used as a vector con-trol. Fortunately, CBs due to toxic components take advan-tages of insecticidal properties. (Dieng et al. 2011, 2013; Murugan et al. 2018). It is evidenced that CBs with thousands of chemicals may be highly toxic and have toxic effects on insects (Dieng et al. 2014). Mondal et al. (2015) reported that chemicals and heavy metals existing in CBs have direct and indirect effects on mosquito larvae; these compounds can eliminate the larvae (Mondal et al. 2015). An important reason for the use of CBs as a vector control is due to the negative effect of nicotine and its derivatives on the insects (Dieng et al. 2014). Therefore, researchers have studied CB application in vector control, as shown in Table 2.

It is evidenced that CBs have negative effects on the tested insects, and this effect was observed on the mosquitoes in the egg decomposition (Dieng et al. 2014) and in the larval stage (Dieng et al. 2013, 2014; Mondal et al. 2015; Murugan et al. 2018). However, with increasing concentrations of CB, more effects were observed (Dieng et al. 2013, 2014; Mondal et al. 2015) and the difference in toxicity observed in various studies was attributed to the difference in the brand of cigarette (Dieng et al. 2013). Murugan et al. (2018) showed that although the extracted chemicals from CB have toxic effects on larval and pupal stages in insects, the toxicity of silver nanostructures de-rived from CB is much higher (see Table 3). In addition, as shown in Table 4, the toxic effects of silver nanostructures pro-duced from CB were reported more severe than those extracted from CB in egg hatchability (Murugan et al. 2018). In addition, this study showed that silver nanostructures inhibited the growth of Bacillus subtilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Salmonella typhi at low concentrations (Murugan et al. 2018). It seems that the toxicity of CBs to insects is true for some birds; in a survey, Su et al. (2017) found that a bird uses CBs in its nest; when the number of live parasites in the nest increased, these birds in-creased the concentration of CBs in their nests (Su et al. 2017). However, further research is needed to ensure that the effect of this substance has no effect on non-target organisms (Dieng et al. 2014).

Use of CBs in activated carbon production

The littered CBs have the potential to be used as a raw material for the activated carbon preparation (Yang et al. 2017); these materials are chemically or physically converted to activated carbon (Yazdi et al. 2012; Yang et al. 2017). Activated carbons produced from CBs can be modified with the use of specific chemicals such as KOH and HNO3 (Masoudi Soltani et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2017), which can improve the carbon sorp-tion capacity through increase of the number of surfactant groups (Masoudi Soltani et al. 2015). Therefore, the activated carbon derived from CB can be used to remove pollutants.

The researchers stated that, given the roughness and the porous structure of the filter rods which provide suitable conditions for the microorganisms attaching and growth, CBs could act as an attached growth bed (Sabzali et al. 2011, 2012). It was reported that CBs not only has the same performance as commercially available carrier, but also it is cheaper (Sabzali et al. 2012). The biofilm

Production of superhydrophobic/superoleophilic fibers from CB

A method to control CB pollution is the conversion of its filter into useful materials. Hence, by soaking a cigarette filter in NaOH solution and then in an ethanolic solution of hexadecyltrimethoxy silane, the cellulose acetate used in the cigarette filter will be converted either to superhydrophobic/ superoleophilic fibers (Ou et al. 2016). Ou et al. (2016) used fibers to remove kerosene from water; it was found that these fibers have over 96% efficiency to absorb kerosene, and this ability was not reduced after 10 cycles. However, the amount of moisture in the collected kerosene in the next cycles was increased; adsorption efficiency was estimated to be in the low level. (Ou et al. 2016).

The paper made of CB pulp had a weight of 71.14 g/m<sup>2</sup> and 0.24-mm thick-ness. Use as metal corrosion inhibitors

Another method to recycle CBs is applying it in order to trap chemical compounds. CBs contain a high amount of nicotine which exhibits anti-corrosion properties, and therefore, it can be used to prevent the corrosion of metal surfaces such as iron (Lucatero et al. 2016). The results of a study published in 2016 showed that water extracted from CBs (WECB) had a signif-icant effect on corrosion inhibition by formation of a surface layer on iron (Lucatero et al. 2016).

Applying as lithium-ion battery membrane separator

The cellulose acetate in the cigarette filters can be separated and accordingly applied for a variety of uses. An application for this purpose is creation of a cellulose-based membrane separator for a high-performance lithium-ion battery. The re-sults of a study on smoked cigarette filter showed that by separating the cellulose acetate from the cigarette filter and removing trapped contaminants by a special process, a nano-fiber membrane separator with high performance and safe for lithium-ion batteries is produced (Huang et al. 2015). Also, in another method that is shown in Fig. 2, an asymmetric supercapacitor with high electrochemical performance was assembled (Wang et al. 2016).

Despite the fact that CB littering bans can be useful (Novotny et al. 1999; Ariza and Leatherman 2012), various studies tried to control CB litters. The most important methods that received much attention from researchers to control CB litters are listed as follows:

a. Labeling

Writing a warning about the non-biodegradability and tox-icity of CB on its packet and also the legal provisions can be effective in CB litters reduction (Novotny et al. 1999).

b. Deposit/return

This method is commonly used for drinking bottles; there-fore, it can also be used for cigarettes in such a way that the extra cost is given back when CBs are delivered after ciga-rettes smoking (Novotny et al. 2009).

c. Waste tax

The enacting legislation taxes on waste materials as an additional cost to cigarettes will both increase prices and re-duce consumption; therefore, this method can be applied to dispose CBs properly (Novotny et al. 1999; Novotny et al. 2009).

d. Fines

Creating fines for CB littering and spending these funds on cigarette butt collection (Novotny et al. 2009; Ariza and Leatherman 2012).

e. Manufacturer’s responsibility

The pressure on factories to make a finance and take prac-tical action against CBs (Smith and Novotny 2011) is

proposed; CBs control can be applied by methods such as extended producer responsibility (EPR) as a postconsumer hazardous waste process. The EPE also used for tire and acid-lead batteries, fluorescent lamps, paint, and insecticides in some areas can be used for CBs. This process will give manufacturers responsibility for CB collection and disposal costs (Ariza and Leatherman 2012).

f. Modification of the cigarette filters

Attempts to produce biodegradable cigarette filters (Novotny et al. 1999; Novotny et al. 2009; Ariza and Leatherman 2012).

g. Education

Extending public education and aspiring feeling responsi-bility in consumers (Novotny et al. 1999; Novotny et al. 2009; Ariza and Leatherman 2012) is a very effective method in CB littering reduction. For instance, a study focused on the smokers’ behavior and attitude revealed that 74% of smokers often disposed CBs as litters (Rath et al. 2012). some studies estimated that the littered CBs ratio is 76% (Patel et al. 2013) and 84% (Lee and Lee 2015) of the smoking disposal way, which indicates the emphasize the effect of the Smoker’s be-havior on this litter. Researchers also placed the emphasizes on the required equipment to increase access to the ashtray in public places (see Fig. 3) (Widmer and Reis 2010; Smith and Novotny 2011; Ariza and Leatherman 2012). In a study con-taining 168 ashtrays, it was revealed that CBs placed in ash-trays was about 50%; many ashtrays were only filled by CBs. The number of needed ashtrays was calculated by calculating the number of CBs thrown in ashtrays and the estimated CB number in the coast (Widmer and Reis 2010).

In addition, given that most of CBs are littered by smokers, CBs durability, and the disper-sion of this debris in urban and public places and even marine environments, introducing solutions for controlling and recycling these hazardous wastes with high dispersion seri-ously is required. Regarding CB littering control, the proposed strategies with the purpose of offsetting costs can be catego-rized into three distinct groups:

a. Establishment of CB littering fines and waste tax for this debris b. Creating a responsibility for the cigarette producers and consumers c. Generic education and awareness of the disadvantages of CBs

It seems that considering the great influence of smokers’ be-haviors on the littered CB quantity, focusing on general educa-tion and the smokers’ behavior as a long-term plan can be con-sidered as an appropriate solution for CB litters controlling. Also, enactment of legislation for CB littering, at least in some areas such as beaches and public places, can be effective as a short-term plan to the littered CB control. However, given CB man-agement cost, it seems that the waste tax legislation on filtered cigarette and the use of manufacturers’ responsibility method would help to manage CB as one of the most important litters.

CB recycling methods

The efforts made by the researchers for CB recycling were based on physical characteristics and chemical properties of CBs. CB recycling methods such as applying them in brick production, asphalt production, vector control, activated car-bon and absorbent production, sound absorbing materials, pa-per production, and as biofilm carrier in wastewater treatment are tested by researchers. In some applications such as bricks or asphalt production, CB processing is not needed and the cigarette can be used in the original shape. However, for other applications, CB is required to be recycled through chemical extraction, pyrolysis, and filter rod extraction. Of course, in most research, disinfection and drying of CBs were carried out before uses. CB recycling methods and their processing are listed in Table 6.

The results of studies show that recycling CBs in different methods can lead to generation of practical products. These studies shown that CBs due to the physical and chemical char-acteristics have a variety of recyclable species. However, the use of CBs in some recycling methods can have undesirable effects on the final product features, which are listed in Table 7.

Information presented in Tables 6 and 7 show that CB recycling is possible due to its structural characteristics or chemicals trapped in its filter. The use of chemicals trapped in CBs and the use of cellulose acetate are the most important targets for CB recycling, while the use of whole CBs in the recycling process without separating its components in a few applications such as building material production was tested. The chemicals trapped in the CB could be extracted with distilled water and chemical solvents. However, applying these chemicals with the aims of vector and metal corrosion controls requires some consideration in terms of public health and the environment (Mondal et al. 2015) (Lucareto et al. 2016). Cigarette filters made up of cellulose acetate are employed in supercapacitor productions after removing chemicals and without any physical deformation. Of note, cellulose acetate was chemically converted in production of pulp and adsorbents (Yazdi et al. 2012), which subsequently leads to emission of polluted gases and wastewater (d’Heni Teixeira et al. 2017). An important aspect to CB recycling in creation building materials such as bricks and asphalt is the quality of the final product. Addition of CBs to raw material affects the characteristics of the final products including strength, thermal conductivity, density, and porosity (Kadir and Mohajerani 2011; Abdul Kadir and Mohajerani 2012; Aeslina and Mohajerani 2012; Kadir et al. 2014; Mohajerani et al. 2016; Murugan et al. 2018). The variation of some char-acteristics is favorable (reduction of brick density); however, decreases in the bricks’ strength are considered as unfavorable characteristics. Furthermore, the recycling process in proper operation conditions such as suitable mixing time leads to produce high-quality products.

Concerns about the recycling of hazardous wastes such as CBs need more attention in terms of environmental issue in recycling plans. For example, it is mentioned that Aedes aegypti has greater tendency to spawn (egg laying) in water containing CBs than without CBs, so the presence of CBs is not a factor in

changing the mosquito laying site (Dieng et al. 2014), as well as the chemicals extracted from CBs did not reduce Anopheles stephensi’s predator activity (Murugan et al. 2018).

The variation in CB recycling methods shown in Table 6 and Table 7 is a potential to integrate several methods to re-duce the concern about the hazardous residue in a particular use of CB. For example, the use of cellulose acetate for the sound absorber production or supercapacitor production after extraction of the trapped chemicals for corrosion inhibitors production or carrier control purpose can reduce the wastewa-ter and hazardous waste production.

Conclusion

The methods of control and recycling cigarette butt (CB) as a major litter was systematically reviewed. Regarding the effect of

smokers’ behavior on CB littering, attempts to modify their behavior have a significant effect on littered CB reduction. Also, applying taxes and penalty and improving the producer responsibility feelings can improve the management of CBs as a hazardous waste. Studies have shown that many characteristics of CBs, even trapped chemical and toxic materials, can be con-sidered as an opportunity to produce appropriate products from this waste. The CB recycling can be defined in three ways:

I. The use of the whole CBs without any processing method, use of this waste in bricks and asphalt production II. The use of chemicals trapped in the CBs for purposes such as vector control and corrosion inhibitors III. The use of cellulose acetate existing in cigarette filter to create suitable materials such as activated carbon, porous sound absorber, and super hydrophobic fibers.

Changes in the final product quality and the pollutant emis-sion are the main problems of the CB recycling methods. However, operation in proper conditions and the integration of various CB recycling methods make it possible to reduce these problems. Nevertheless, environmental aspects of the CB recycling as a hazardous waste need further study.
